# Delivery models

Same promise, three vehicles, three different offers. Whether you do the work, guide someone doing
it, or hand them what they need to do it alone is not a packaging choice made after the offer exists
— it is the decision that determines what the offer is worth, what it costs to fulfil, and whether
this particular buyer can say yes at all.

The three levels are conventionally named do-it-yourself, done-with-you and done-for-you — the
vocabulary Alex Hormozi's material uses and the one clients already have. What follows is a different
way of reading them than the usual pros-and-cons list.

## Read the three by what each one transfers

| | Who does the work | Who carries the risk of it going wrong | Who controls the schedule | What the buyer has afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Do-it-yourself** | The buyer | The buyer | The buyer | The method, and whatever they built with it |
| **Done-with-you** | Both, at a defined seam | Shared, and the seam is where disputes happen | Negotiated, usually a cadence | The method, plus a working instance, plus the capability |
| **Done-for-you** | You | You | You | A result, and a dependency on you to maintain it |

Those four columns explain most of what the pros-and-cons framing gets at, and they explain the
things it misses. **Schedule control is why do-it-yourself completion rates are low** — nothing
external ever makes the next step happen. **Risk transfer is most of what done-for-you is actually
priced on** — the buyer is paying to stop being the person who is blamed. And the last column is why
a technically-minded buyer sometimes prefers the middle option at a higher effort cost: he wants his
team to be able to do it next time without calling you.

Pick against the buyer's real constraint, not against margin preference:

- Where the buyer lacks the skill and the outcome matters more than the learning, do-it-yourself
  fails regardless of price. It will be bought and not completed, which produces a refund request or,
  worse, a quiet non-renewal and no case study.
- Where the buyer wants to retain the capability, done-for-you is a downgrade being sold as an
  upgrade.
- Where speed is the binding constraint from `value-equation.md`, every step toward done-for-you buys
  time delay directly, and that is usually the cheapest place to spend budget.

## Hybrids work at a named seam, not as a blend

The useful hybrids are specific about where the handover sits: you do the setup and they run it; they
do the work and you review it at fixed points; you build the first one together and they build the
rest. Each of those is a sentence a buyer can picture.

"A bit of both" is not. A hybrid without a stated seam is where scope creep starts, because both
parties assume the other has the ambiguous middle, and the ambiguous middle is always larger than
either of them expected.

## Effort reduction is a design activity, not a polish pass

Walk the buyer's path from the moment they decide to the moment they hold the first result, and mark
every place work sits on their side. That walk is the input to most of the good ideas in an offer,
and it produces three kinds of change:

- **Work you can take off them entirely.** Pre-filling something, doing the setup, arriving with the
  first draft already made. Each one moves the offer a step toward done-for-you at that specific
  point without changing the model overall.
- **Decisions you can make for them.** Every choice a buyer has to make is a place they can stall. A
  recommended default with an override is nearly always better than a genuine menu.
- **Blank pages you can pre-fill.** Starting from a structure is a categorically different task from
  starting from nothing, and this is the cheapest effort reduction available in most offers.

And one that is easy to miss and belongs here rather than in a copy pass: **specifying an unspecified
inclusion is itself an effort reduction.** For as long as nobody has said what a portal, tracker or
workbook actually does, the buyer cannot tell whether it helps him or becomes his responsibility, so
he prices it as a burden. Scoping it changes nothing about the delivery and flips the sign of the
item. See `value-equation.md`'s effort section.

## Engineering time-to-first-value

The single highest-leverage delivery change on a long engagement is making the *earliest* milestone
produce something complete, and saying so.

The move: identify the first point at which the buyer holds something finished — a written plan, a
working configuration, a decision they can act on — and make the offer promise *that*, at that date,
in addition to the eventual outcome. A long wait becomes a short wait followed by a supervised
extension, and nothing about the format changed.

Two checks before proposing it. Is the early milestone genuinely self-sufficient, or does it only
make sense once the later part lands? And does something in the existing material already do this job
while pointing at a date that no longer exists? The second case is common after a format change and
the fix is re-pointing rather than writing.

## Productizing a service

A service becomes a product when four things are true: the scope is fixed and written, the timeline
is fixed, the inclusions are the same every time, and there is a defined checkpoint where both sides
agree it is on track. Add a name and it is sellable as a thing rather than as hours.

What that buys: a price that does not get re-negotiated per client, a sale that does not need a
bespoke proposal, and a repeatable delivery that generates comparable case studies — which is the
compounding benefit, because comparable results are the ones that raise perceived likelihood for the
next buyer.

What it costs: the ability to accommodate. Every accommodation granted quietly un-productizes the
product, and the second client who gets one has established that the scope is negotiable. Decide up
front what is genuinely fixed, and price the exceptions as change orders rather than absorbing them.

An offer ladder — an entry rung, the main offer, a premium rung — is the natural extension, and
`offer-angles.md` covers when to build one and when it is premature.

## By register

Which model reads as premium, and which reads as the seller offloading work, varies more than the
delivery itself does. Read only the row the brief named.

| Register | Reads as premium | Reads badly, and why |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative B2B (DACH) | Done-for-you with the scope written out — competence demonstrated by knowing exactly what you will and will not do. A defined checkpoint matters as much as the deliverable. | Do-it-yourself at a serious price, which reads as the seller not having a method of his own. Also any hybrid whose seam is unstated, since the buyer is the one who has to defend the scope internally. |
| Technical / industrial | Done-with-you, frequently ahead of done-for-you: the practitioner wants the capability to stay in his own team, and a vendor dependency is a risk on his register, not a convenience. | Done-for-you pitched as removing work he did not want removed. Ask before assuming that effort reduction is what he is buying. |
| Consumer / lifestyle | Done-for-you as the aspiration and the upsell; do-it-yourself sells honestly where the doing is itself the pleasure. | Done-with-you sold as convenience when it is actually homework. Name the commitment, in hours, before the purchase. |
| Mission / nonprofit | Done-with-you, because building the organisation's own capacity is the register's stated value and outlasting your involvement is the point. | Done-for-you framed as rescue, which creates a dependency the organisation cannot fund next year and reads as such to anyone experienced in the sector. |
| Direct-response | Do-it-yourself at volume with speed engineered in; done-for-you as the high-ticket rung above it. | A middle rung whose commitment is not stated up front — this buyer's tolerance for discovering ongoing work after purchase is the lowest of the five. |
