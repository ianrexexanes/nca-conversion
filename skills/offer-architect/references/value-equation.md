# The Value Equation, used as a diagnostic

Four variables decide whether a buyer thinks an offer is worth its price. Two of them raise the
verdict and two lower it: what the buyer ends up with and whether they believe they will actually
get it, set against how long they wait for it and what they give up beyond the money.
The frame is Alex Hormozi's, from *$100M Offers*, and the four variable names below are his; what
this file adds is how to read a real offer against them without kidding yourself.

**This is a diagnostic, not a scorecard.** Do not produce a composite figure out of a hundred. A
composite averages one fatal problem with three healthy variables and hands back a number that
conceals which one is actually stopping the sale. Rank the four against each other instead and name
the **binding constraint** — the single variable currently doing the most damage. Then design against
that one. Two consecutive passes at two different variables beat one pass that touches all four
lightly.

---

## Dream outcome

What the buyer is actually buying, which is usually a level above the thing they asked for.

The test that finds it: ask who signs, then ask what that person's own problem is. A department head
buying training for his team is not buying technique for one attendee — he is buying a team that hits
a number he is personally accountable for. An offer that promises the attendee's win to the signer is
selling the right thing to the wrong chair.

**Read the buyer's role before the buyer's request.** The request comes from whoever filled in the
form; the outcome that gets a purchase approved belongs to whoever carries the consequence of not
solving it.

### The failure this variable hides: the offer moves and the promise doesn't

The most common finding on a real offer is that somebody genuinely improved the product and never
changed a word of what it claims. New components get added that all point at a bigger, better-aimed
outcome, while the page still promises the small one it promised a year ago.

This matters because it is the cheapest of the four variables to fix and it is constantly mistaken
for the most expensive. **Separate the copy gap from the offer gap before proposing anything.** A copy
gap means the offer already delivers the outcome and nothing says so — an editing job. An offer gap
means nothing in the product actually produces the outcome being claimed — a build job. Proposing a
build when you have a copy gap wastes the client's quarter; proposing an edit when you have an offer
gap ships a promise the product cannot keep.

## Perceived likelihood of achievement

**This variable is the buyer's estimate at the moment of purchase. It is not the programme's actual
efficacy, and the two come apart constantly.** An offer that works can score badly here, and knowing
which one you are looking at decides whether the fix is proof or product.

What raises it, in rough order of how hard the artefact is to fake:

- Something the buyer can verify himself, alone, in a few minutes — a named practitioner whose role
  is checkable on the public record, a published figure with a unit and a date, a document he can
  download.
- A ratio or an arithmetic fact that explains *why* the outcome is more likely here — a stated
  participant-to-instructor number, a stated caseload, a stated turnaround. A number that carries its
  own mechanism outperforms a number that only asserts a result.
- The buyer's own material used inside the delivery. A buyer estimating whether something works *for
  him specifically* raises his estimate when the work runs on his own inputs rather than a worked
  example.
- Anything that converts "a thing I attend" into "a thing prepared for me" — an intake questionnaire,
  a pre-read, a diagnostic that happens before delivery starts. These are routinely the cheapest
  item on a list of proposed changes and routinely the most underrated.

What lowers it, and what to check for:

- No proof at all, by construction, because the thing has never run in this form. Say this plainly in
  the diagnosis rather than treating it as a gap to be papered over — it is a real constraint on what
  the offer can claim, and it usually points at a pilot rather than a launch.
- A market convention the offer omits. Where every benchmarked competitor issues something — a
  certificate, a report, a written summary — its absence is not neutral, it is conspicuous.
- **An inclusion the business cannot yet deliver.** This is the expensive one. An item promised in
  the stack that has no launch date, no owner and no specification is a likelihood *liability*, not
  an asset, and it becomes one the day a buyer asks about it. Check deliverability before crediting
  any inclusion in the diagnosis, and check it against a dated record rather than an intention.
- A claim the page makes that its own assets contradict. Three named experts and one photograph is
  the general shape of this: the offer asserts a plurality and the evidence shows a single person.

`../../audience-research/references/register-profiles.md` decides what counts as proof for this
particular reader. The variable is the same in every register; the artefact that satisfies it is not.

## Time delay

How long from paying to getting something, and how long from paying to getting all of it. **Measure
those two separately.** They are different numbers, they move independently, and collapsing them is
how a fixable problem gets reported as a structural one.

A useful shape for the finding, when a format has changed:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase | | |
| First value delivered | | |
| Complete value delivered | | |
| Elapsed, purchase to complete | | |

Two consequences fall out of that table and are almost never priced:

- **A deadline drifts away from what it is a deadline for.** An early-commitment window that sits a
  long way in front of the completion date asks the buyer for a long hold, and the further apart the
  two dates sit the less force the deadline has.
- **A long delivery window can cross an accounting boundary.** Asking a buyer to book this year's
  budget against a benefit that mostly lands next year is a time-delay problem wearing an accounting
  costume, and it surfaces as a budget objection rather than as a scheduling one.

**The diagnosis usually ends by pointing at the first milestone, not the last.** Where the two
numbers above are far apart, the cheapest available move on this variable is nearly always to make
the earliest milestone stand on its own rather than to compress the whole delivery — which is a
change to the delivery's shape, not to the diagnosis. `delivery-models.md`'s
*Engineering time-to-first-value* section owns that move and the two checks that decide whether it
is available here.

## Effort and sacrifice

Everything the purchase costs the buyer besides money: their own hours, decisions, learning, absence
from their desk, coordination with people who did not ask for this, and the social risk of having
recommended it internally.

Three things to look for that a feature list will not show you:

- **Count the discrete impositions, not the total hours.** Two separate one-day absences in two
  different months cost more than one two-day block, because each one is its own travel decision, its
  own diary negotiation and its own round of covering the desk — even though the hours are identical.
  The same arithmetic multiplies when a signer is sending several people.
- **An unquantified ask gets priced at its maximum.** "Participants keep working on their own case
  between sessions" is an open-ended commitment, and a buyer with no stated figure supplies the worst
  one he can imagine. Naming a rough number always prices lower than naming none, so the single line
  the offer owes this variable is usually *how much of your time this actually takes*.
- **An unspecified asset is an effort adder until it is specified.** A dashboard, a portal, a workbook
  or a tracker is an effort reducer in principle and an effort adder in practice for as long as
  nobody has said what it does. The buyer cannot tell whether it is something that helps him or
  another system he is now responsible for keeping current. The same item flips sign depending on
  framing: preparation the provider does for the buyer reduces effort, homework the buyer owes before
  he has even attended adds it.

Note which items moved *down* too. Removing an overnight stay or an evening obligation is a genuine
reduction for a local buyer and a small increase for a distant one; say which case dominates for this
ICP rather than reporting the change as unqualified.

## Arguing a structural change instead of assuming its sign

The hardest judgement in this diagnosis is what a change to the *shape* of an offer does to the
verdict — a gap inserted between two halves, a phase added, a delivery split across a boundary. These
are routinely assumed to be improvements by whoever designed them, and they are not automatically
anything. Build the argument in two steps and state both.

**Step one: what is the buyer's default reading, with no framing at all?** Absent any explanation, a
long gap inside a programme reads to a buyer exactly the way every deferred initiative he has ever run
reads — *I will have forgotten the first half by the time the second one starts.* That instinct is
well founded, it is the same instinct behind "let's pick this up after the summer," and it is what
the offer gets for free if the copy says nothing. On the default reading, the change is
likelihood-negative.

**Step two: what would change that reading, and does it exist visibly at the moment of purchase?**
Not promised, not planned — visible, on the page, before money changes hands. Usually three things
would do it: a dated event the buyer can picture himself sitting in, an artefact he can imagine using
in the interval, and a stated cadence, because an unspecified working period reads as either nothing
or as unlimited and both estimates are bad ones.

Then count how many of those actually exist today. If the answer is one, and it exists as a sentence
in a message rather than as anything a buyer can see, the honest finding is that **the change is
negative as the buyer will encounter it, and positive only once the missing pieces are built.** That
is a finding about sequence, not a reason to reverse the format — and it is a different, more useful
thing to hand a client than either "this is good" or "this is bad."

Two smaller notes on this pattern. A secondary effect that operates *after* the sale — money already
spent making abandonment harder, say — is real but does nothing for a variable measured at the moment
of purchase, so keep it separate rather than counting it twice. And a structural change's sign
sometimes turns on a commercial term nobody has confirmed, such as when payment is actually
collected; where it does, that dependency is part of the finding rather than a detail to settle in
passing — carry it under the carried-assumption rule in `../SKILL.md`.

## Mapping the obstacles

Once the binding constraint is named, enumerate what actually stands between this buyer and the
outcome. Do this before proposing anything: an obstacle nobody wrote down arrives later as an
objection, and by then it costs a section of page copy instead of a line in a table.

Work from the buyer's situation rather than from a generic list — what they don't know, what they
can't do without help, what they have tried already and why it didn't hold, what they cannot
authorise alone, what they will be blamed for if it fails. Aim for more obstacles than feel
necessary; the ones that get cut are cheap to cut and the one that gets missed is not.

Then convert every one of them into something that removes it, plus the vehicle that carries it, in a
table the offer document takes forward:

| Obstacle | What removes it | How it reaches the buyer | Which variable that moves |
|---|---|---|---|

The last column is what keeps this honest. A proposed inclusion that cannot be tied to one of the
four variables is a feature somebody wanted to build, and it belongs in the backlog rather than in
the offer. `delivery-models.md` decides the third column; `value-stack-and-bonuses.md` decides which
rows become stack lines and which become bonuses.
