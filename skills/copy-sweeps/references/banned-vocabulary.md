# Vocabulary and structural tells

This file stands on its own. It assumes no brief, no register decision and no other file in this
plugin — it is what `conversion-page-build` and `offer-architect` fall back to when no `humanizer`
skill is installed, and it is written so that someone who opens it cold can use it in one pass.

Its scope is deliberately narrow: **words and rhythm.** Whether a claim is worth making, provable,
specific or aimed at the right reader is a different problem, handled by `seven-sweeps.md`. Prose
can clear every rule here and still be arguing for the wrong thing.

Two things about how to use it.

**A list without reasons does not survive paraphrase.** Every entry below says why the word fails,
because the failure is what generalises. Ban *robust* and the writer produces *rock-solid*. Explain
that the word asserts a quality the sentence never demonstrates and the writer stops reaching for
the whole family.

**Nothing here is banned in a sentence that earns it.** *Very* in reported speech, *comprehensive*
describing something genuinely exhaustive, a hedge in front of a claim that genuinely is uncertain —
all fine. The tell is the reflex, not the word.

---

## Part 1 — Words that fail, and why

### Adjectives that assert instead of showing

The shared defect: the word makes a quality claim and supplies no way to check it, so the reader
either accepts it unexamined or discounts the sentence. Replacing one with a synonym changes
nothing. Replace it with the fact that made you reach for it.

| Word | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| robust | Names a strength without saying what it withstands. |
| seamless | Describes an absence of friction the reader has not experienced and cannot verify. |
| comprehensive | A completeness claim with no boundary — complete relative to what? |
| innovative | Novelty asserted by the seller is the one kind of novelty that carries no weight. |
| cutting-edge | Dates instantly, and every competitor claims it in the same week. |
| world-class | Implies a ranking that does not exist and could not be produced on request. |
| best-in-class | Same defect, with an invented class attached to make it sound measured. |
| powerful | Says the tool does a lot without naming one thing it does. |
| game-changing | Predicts a consequence the seller is not in a position to observe. |
| revolutionary | Reads as fraudulent in every conservative register and as filler in the rest. |
| state-of-the-art | Borrowed from procurement documents, where it also means nothing. |
| unparalleled | Asserts that no comparison exists, which is a claim about the whole market. |
| bespoke | Almost always describes a standard process with a form at the front. |
| holistic | Signals breadth in order to avoid naming any specific part. |

### Verbs that displace a plainer one

The shared defect: a longer verb substituted for a short one, buying formality and losing precision.
The plain verb is not merely simpler — it is usually more specific about what actually happened.

| Word | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| leverage | Conceals what was done with the thing being leveraged. |
| utilise | Identical in meaning to *use* and only longer. |
| facilitate | Removes the agent — somebody either did it or did not. |
| delve | Almost never written by a person who is not being paid by the word. |
| unlock | Implies a barrier that the sentence never establishes. |
| elevate | A vertical metaphor doing the work a measurable change should do. |
| empower | Claims a transfer of capability without saying what the reader can now do. |
| streamline | An efficiency claim with no before figure attached. |
| spearhead | Borrowed heroism; *led* is both shorter and more credible. |
| harness | Same family as *leverage*, same evasion. |
| navigate | Turns a specific difficulty into a vague journey. |
| foster | Institutional. Nobody says it aloud about their own work. |
| underscore | Reviewer's vocabulary, not a seller's. |
| optimise | Used without a metric, it means *changed*. |

### Intensifiers that measure nothing

The shared defect: these words modify the sentence's confidence without adding information, and a
reader who notices one starts discounting the ones around it. Deleting them almost never weakens a
sentence; where it does, the sentence needed a number rather than an intensifier.

| Word | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| very | Adds emphasis to an adjective that should have been a stronger word or a figure. |
| really | Conversational filler that reads as pleading in written copy. |
| incredibly | Literally claims disbelief, which is not the reaction you want. |
| extremely | Same function as *very*, one notch louder, equally unmeasured. |
| truly | Implies the surrounding claims were less than true. |
| absolutely | Adds nothing to a binary — something is or is not. |
| significantly | Sounds statistical and is not; the reader wants the figure. |
| dramatically | A judgement about the reader's reaction, made in advance for them. |
| substantially | Corporate hedging that pretends to be a quantity. |

### Hedges that quietly withdraw the claim

The shared defect: the sentence keeps its shape while giving up its content. Stack two of these and
the reader cannot tell whether anything was asserted. This is the most damaging group in
conservative and technical writing, where the copy is already understated and the hedge takes it
below the threshold of saying anything.

| Word or phrase | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| can help | Concedes it might not, and might not be help. |
| is designed to | Describes an intention, not a result. |
| aims to | The same, from further away. |
| may allow | Two hedges in three words. |
| up to | Names the best case and lets the reader assume the rest. |
| potentially | Every claim is potentially true; the word is free. |
| arguably | Signals that the writer expects an argument and will not have it. |
| in many cases | An unstated denominator wearing a caveat's clothes. |
| tends to | Fine in analysis, evasive in a promise. |
| we believe | Downgrades a fact to an opinion, usually for no reason. |

### Nouns with no referent

The shared defect: a category noun standing where the thing itself belongs, so the reader finishes
the sentence without learning what is being sold.

| Word | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| solutions | The single most common substitute for naming the product. |
| offerings | Same evasion, one register up. |
| capabilities | A list of these tells the reader nothing about what they get. |
| synergies | Survives only in documents nobody reads voluntarily. |
| ecosystem | Borrowed grandeur for what is usually a set of integrations. |
| journey | Sequences a purchase as a narrative to avoid naming the steps. |
| space (as in "the X space") | An industry named without being named. |
| touchpoint | Describes a conversation, an email or a page without saying which. |

### Openers and connectors that announce the machine

The shared defect: these do not merely bore, they identify the writer. A reader who hits one starts
reading for authorship rather than for content, and everything after it is discounted.

| Phrase | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| It's worth noting that | If it were worth noting, note it. |
| In today's fast-paced world | Says nothing, and is the single most recognised opener of its kind. |
| At its core | Announces a simplification instead of performing one. |
| Let's dive in | Frames the reader as an audience at a webinar. |
| In the ever-evolving landscape of | Scene-setting for a scene that is never used. |
| That said / That being said | A pivot with no content, usually reversing a claim made one line earlier. |
| In conclusion | Nobody says this at the end of anything they wrote for a person. |
| Ultimately | Filler occupying the position where the point belongs. |
| At the end of the day | The same, in the pub. |

### The em dash question

The em dash is the most-cited single marker of machine-written prose, and treating it as forbidden
is a mistake: it is a legitimate mark and this repository uses it. **The tell is density and
uniformity**, not presence — several per paragraph, all doing the same parenthetical job, in copy
whose other punctuation never varies. A house style that bans it outright is a house style; follow
it where one exists. Where none does, vary the punctuation rather than the count.

## Part 2 — Structural tells

Copy can pass every table above and still read as machine-written, because the giveaway is often
rhythm rather than vocabulary. These are the patterns readers notice without being able to name.

**Paragraphs of uniform length.** Human writing has a paragraph that runs long because the thought
did, and a two-line one because that was all there was to say. Six paragraphs of four sentences each
is a shape no writer produces by accident, and it reads as generated even when every sentence in it
is good. Fix by letting the content set the length: merge the ones that share a thought, and cut the
ones that were padded to match their neighbours.

**One paragraph per topic, in the order the topics were listed.** A section that announces three
things and then hands each exactly one paragraph in the announced order is executing a plan rather
than making an argument. Real argument doubles back, spends longer on the contested point, and
disposes of the easy one in a clause. Fix by asking which of the three actually matters and giving
it the space.

**Tricolon on repeat.** Three-item lists are a good rhythm, which is why the reflex overuses them
until every list has exactly three members and the third is padding. The count is the tell: if three
sentences in a row each carry a trio, at least one of them is inventing an item. Fix by finding the
one that was added for cadence and deleting it.

**Negate, then elevate.** *It's not just X, it's Y.* *This isn't about X — it's about Y.* The
construction promises depth by rejecting a claim nobody made, and it is close to a signature. It
also usually contains no information, since X and Y turn out to be the same thing at two levels of
abstraction. Fix by deleting the negation and keeping whatever Y actually says.

**Hedges stacked on hedges.** *This approach can potentially help many teams to significantly reduce
what may be a substantial cost.* Each hedge is defensible on its own; four in one sentence leave the
reader unable to state what was claimed. Fix by keeping the one uncertainty that is real and
deleting the rest — and if the claim is genuinely unsupported, say so rather than dissolving it into
qualifiers.

**The summarising close.** A final paragraph that restates the section just read, adding nothing.
Written prose gets to trust the reader's memory across a short section. Fix by cutting it; if
something in it was new, it belonged earlier.

**Symmetrical scaffolding.** Every section built to the same template — a bolded lead-in, a colon, a
list, a closing line. Once a reader detects the template, they read the templates and skip the
content. Fix by breaking the shape wherever the content does not need it.

**Vocabulary that never varies.** The same connective, the same sentence opening, the same verb for
the same action, throughout. This is not consistency; it is the absence of choice, and it flattens
everything to the same emphasis.

## Using this file on its own

Three passes, in this order, on any draft:

1. **Read for the tables.** Mark, do not fix. Fixing while reading turns into rewriting, and the
   point is to see how many marks accumulate — a page with forty is a page with a rhythm problem,
   not forty word problems.
2. **Cut.** Start with the intensifiers and hedges, which almost always delete cleanly, then the
   assertive adjectives, which need the underlying fact put back in their place. Where you cannot
   supply the fact, the sentence probably should not be making the claim.
3. **Read once more for the structural tells**, which only become visible after the vocabulary work
   is done, and which are the ones a reader will actually notice.

Then check that nothing was quietly lost. Deleting a hedge can convert a careful claim into an
overstatement, and that is a worse defect than the hedge was.
