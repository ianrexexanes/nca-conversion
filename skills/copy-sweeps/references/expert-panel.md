# Expert panel — an optional mode, not a second skill

The seven passes work at the level of the sentence. None of them is permitted to ask whether the
argument being made is the right argument, which means all seven can pass cleanly over copy that is
selling the wrong thing to the wrong person in a beautifully clear way. This mode asks that question
by putting the copy in front of practitioners whose published positions disagree with each other.

Run it on copy where being wrong is expensive: a page about to receive paid traffic, a launch
sequence, a proposal, a pitch that will be repeated in meetings. Do not run it on a caption.

## The grounding rule

**A panellist speaks only from a position they demonstrably held, in a work that can be named.**
Nothing else counts. If you cannot state, for the question actually on the table, what this person
argued and where they argued it, that seat stays empty for this review. Three seats grounded beats
seven seats where four are guesses.

The reason is asymmetric damage. A missing Ogilvy take costs the review one perspective. An invented
Ogilvy take costs it more than that, because it arrives wearing decades of authority and gets
believed — the reader has no way to separate the two, and neither does the person who wrote it a
week later. A fabricated position from a famous name is not a weaker version of a real one; it is a
different kind of object, and it will be quoted onward.

Four rules follow, and they are the whole discipline:

1. **Name the work in the take itself.** Not "Ogilvy would say" but "Ogilvy's position in *Ogilvy on
   Advertising* is X, which applied here means Y." The named work is what makes the claim checkable
   by whoever reads the review.
2. **Paraphrase the position; quote only what you can verify word for word.** A remembered quotation
   is the most common way an invented position enters, because it feels like evidence.
3. **Never put an opinion about this specific business in a real person's mouth.** The panellist
   applies a framework to the copy. They have not seen it and have no view on it.
4. **Most of these seven are alive and their positions move.** Ogilvy and Schwartz
   are closed sets — what they held is what they published. For Dunford, Sutherland,
   Hormozi, Sharp and Godin, a book is a snapshot: where the question is time-sensitive — a
   platform, a channel, a current practice — check for something recent rather than reasoning from
   a book, and say when the position you are citing dates from.

**Where the position and the question do not meet, say so and reason by stated analogy.** Byron
Sharp's evidence base is mass-market repeat-purchase categories; applied to a niche B2B page with
forty possible buyers in the world, his laws are outside the conditions they were measured under.
That is worth saying in the take. It is not worth papering over.

The seven below were each checked against a named work when this file was written. Anyone adding an
eighth owes the same check — the entry is not finished until the work is named.

## The bench

Seven, chosen so that their answers to the same page genuinely conflict.

### David Ogilvy — the headline and the length of the argument

*Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983); *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963).

**Position held.** That the headline consumes most of the attention an advertisement will ever get,
and should be budgeted for accordingly — his own formulation put readership of the headline at
several times that of the body copy. And, against the prevailing instinct then and now, that for
considered and expensive purchases long copy outsells short: he argued that prospects who are
seriously in the market are hungry for information, and that industrial advertisements with long
copy were read more thoroughly rather than less.

**Puts to the copy.** Has the first line done the work the whole page depends on? And is this copy
short because the argument is complete, or because someone assumed nobody reads?

**Wrong panellist for.** Anything about scrolling, form fields, mobile behaviour or measurement. His
evidence is print and television.

### Eugene Schwartz — what the reader already knows

*Breakthrough Advertising* (1966).

**Position held.** That copy does not manufacture desire; it takes desire that already exists in a
market and connects it to a particular product. From that follow the two staged frameworks the book
is known for: five levels of reader awareness, from unaware through problem-aware, solution-aware
and product-aware to most-aware, which decide what an opening may assume; and market sophistication,
which tracks how many competing claims this reader has already sat through in this category and
therefore whether a plain claim still works or whether the mechanism has to lead.

**Puts to the copy.** What does this reader already know, and how many times has he heard this exact
promise from somebody else? A stage-one headline in a stage-four market is the most common way a
page fails while reading perfectly well.

**Wrong panellist for.** Trust mechanics and proof artifacts — his frameworks are about the shape of
the claim, not about evidence for it.

### April Dunford — what the reader is comparing it to

*Obviously Awesome* (2019); *Sales Pitch* (2023).

**Position held.** That positioning is context: the same product reads as obvious or as baffling
depending on the frame it is placed in, and the frame is a deliberate choice rather than an
inheritance. Her components start from competitive alternatives — what the buyer would actually do
if this did not exist, including doing nothing — and run through the attributes only you have, the
value those produce, the customers who care most about that value, and the market category that
makes all of it legible.

**Puts to the copy.** What is the reader comparing this to while reading, and does the page know? A
page that never names the alternative leaves the comparison to be made silently, in the reader's
head, against something the seller has not chosen.

**Wrong panellist for.** Low-consideration impulse purchases. Her work is shaped by B2B software
buying and it assumes a buyer who is evaluating.

### Rory Sutherland — is this a perception problem

*Alchemy* (2019).

**Position held.** That perceived experience is frequently cheaper to change than the underlying
reality, and that businesses systematically overspend on the reality. His Eurostar argument is the
standing example: on the sum he cites for speeding the train up, a small percentage
of that budget spent on the journey itself would have bought more satisfaction than the saved
minutes did. Alongside it, the maxim he states in that book as *"the opposite of a good idea can
also be a good idea"* — a
claim about testing rather than about whimsy, since two contradictory approaches can each work and
argument alone will not tell you which one does here.

**Puts to the copy.** Is the problem here actually a perception problem with a cheap psychological
fix, rather than the substantive problem the page is straining to solve? And what would the
deliberately illogical version of this page look like?

**Wrong panellist for.** Delivering a verdict. His contribution generates hypotheses, and a
hypothesis should exit into a test rather than into shipped copy — see
`../../conversion-page-audit/references/test-design.md`.

### Alex Hormozi — which variable the copy is arguing

*$100M Offers* (2021).

**Position held.** That perceived value can be read as four variables — what the buyer wants, how
convinced they are that they will get it, the wait before they do, and the disruption they absorb
along the way — and that most offers argue the first of those and leave the other three sitting at
whatever the buyer assumes. And that an offer should be constructed deliberately, with its
guarantee and its name as construction steps rather than trimmings.

**Puts to the copy.** Which of the four is this page actually arguing, and which is it leaving the
reader to assume the worst about? Time and effort are the two usually left blank.

**Wrong panellist for.** Register. The volume that is native and expected in `Direct-response` is a
credibility problem in `Conservative B2B (DACH)`. Take the diagnosis, leave the decibels. The
four-variable analysis itself belongs to `../../offer-architect/references/value-equation.md`; this
seat only asks whether the copy reflects one that was already done.

### Byron Sharp — the buyer who is not reading yet

*How Brands Grow* (2010).

**Position held.** That brands grow mainly by reaching more buyers rather than by extracting more
from existing ones, and that most of those buyers are light and infrequent. That mental availability
— being thought of at the moment a category need arises — and physical availability do most of the
work. That distinctiveness, in the sense of assets a buyer recognises instantly, matters more than
meaningful differentiation. And the double jeopardy pattern, in which a brand with the smaller
customer base is also bought marginally less often by the customers it does have — the two
disadvantages arrive together, which is why a loyalty programme is usually treating a symptom.

**Puts to the copy.** Would a category buyer who is not in the market today recognise whose page
this is? Is the copy over-invested in persuading people who are already convinced?

**Wrong panellist for.** Small closed markets. The laws come from large repeat-purchase categories;
say so explicitly rather than applying them to a page with a countable buyer universe.

### Seth Godin — is any of this worth repeating

*Purple Cow* (2003); *Permission Marketing* (1999); *This Is Marketing* (2018).

**Position held.** That remarkable means literally worth making a remark about, and that the
unremarkable is not weaker marketing but invisible marketing. That the useful target is the smallest
viable audience — the smallest group whose enthusiasm sustains the thing — rather than the largest
reachable one. And that attention taken without permission is a cost to the person it is taken from,
where permission granted is anticipated, personal and relevant.

**Puts to the copy.** Is there one sentence here that somebody would repeat to a colleague? And who
precisely is this for — not the segment, the person?

**Wrong panellist for.** Chairing a conversion review. He argues against optimisation as an end, so
on a page whose job is to convert traffic that already exists he is the dissenter, and seating him
as the deciding voice mistakes a useful objection for a verdict.

## Seating

Three seats for a normal review, five when the copy carries the whole launch. Two rules:

1. **Pick for the question, not for fame.** Copy whose problem might be the claim: Schwartz, Dunford,
   Hormozi. Copy whose problem might be that nobody will notice it: Godin, Sharp, Sutherland. Copy
   whose problem is that it does not convince: Ogilvy, Hormozi, plus whoever will argue the opposite.
2. **One seat must be occupied by somebody who disagrees with where the copy is already heading.**
   A bench that endorses the draft has told you nothing you did not bring with you. Sharp against
   Godin on whether reach or resonance binds, Sutherland against Hormozi on whether the fix is
   psychological or structural, and Godin against anything optimised into blandness are the
   productive collisions.

## Output

Open with one line that says plainly what the reader is looking at — published positions applied to
this copy by whoever ran the review, and not the judgement of the people named. Say it once and do
not repeat it per seat.

Then, per seat: the position and the work it comes from, what it says about this specific copy in
two or three paragraphs, and one sentence naming the single change that panellist would make.

Then the part that carries the value — **where the bench disagrees.** Two or three real conflicts,
each stated as the underlying trade-off rather than as a difference of opinion, and each followed by
what evidence would actually settle it. A conflict that no evidence could settle is a matter of
taste and should be labelled as one.

Close with a recommendation that says which conflict you resolved and why, and which panellist's
objection you are keeping as a thing to watch after the copy ships.

## Failure modes

- **The take that would survive a name swap.** If the paragraph reads the same with a different
  panellist's name on it, no position was applied and the seat is decorative.
- **A quotation nobody can source.** Cut it. The paraphrase was doing the work anyway.
- **Panel notes overriding register.** A `Direct-response` panellist's instinct applied unchanged to
  `Conservative B2B (DACH)` copy is a register violation with a famous name attached. The brief's
  register outranks the bench.
- **Seven seats because seven were available.** Match the bench to what is at stake; a headline does
  not need a full panel, and a long review nobody finishes is worse than three sharp takes.
- **Running the panel instead of the passes.** The panel does not fix sentences. It decides
  direction; `seven-sweeps.md` executes.
