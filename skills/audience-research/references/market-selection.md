# Market selection

The ICP section of the brief names a company profile. Before that profile can be written well, the
market it sits inside has to be chosen deliberately — a strong offer aimed at a weak market
underperforms a weak offer aimed at a starving one, and no amount of copy quality closes that gap.
This file covers how to evaluate a market and narrow it to a sub-market before writing the ICP.

## Four signals that indicate a strong market

A market worth building an offer for shows most of these at once. None alone is sufficient, and a
market can look strong on one axis and be dead on another.

**Pain.** The problem costs the buyer something they can name — money, time, status, risk — right
now, not hypothetically. A market where the problem is mildly annoying rather than costly produces
prospects who nod at the pitch and never act on it, because nothing is forcing a decision.

**Purchasing power.** The buyer can actually pay for the solution, and the amount they'd pay is
worth the effort of serving them. A market in real pain but with no budget line for solving it — a
common trap in mission-driven or early-stage buyer segments — produces engaged, sympathetic
prospects and no revenue.

**Ease of targeting.** The market can be found and reached at a reasonable cost — they gather in
identifiable places (a platform, an industry event, a searchable job title, a trade publication)
rather than being scattered and undiscoverable. A market that is real, in pain, and able to pay but
cannot be found or addressed as a group is not yet a workable market for a single offer.

**Growing.** The market is expanding or at minimum stable, not shrinking. A market in structural
decline can still convert well today and still be the wrong bet, because the same offer gets harder
to sell every quarter for reasons no amount of better copy fixes.

## Narrowing to a sub-market

"Small business owners" or "people who want to get in shape" are not markets an offer can be built
for — they're categories containing dozens of markets with different pain, different purchasing
power, and different language. A sub-market is the point where the four signals above can actually
be evaluated, because, for instance, "SaaS founders between $1M and $5M ARR who just missed a fundraise" has a
specific, nameable pain and a specific, findable location, where "small business owners" does not.

Narrow along whichever of these actually separates the buyers who convert from the ones who don't
— not all of them, and not by default:

- **Demographic or firmographic** — company size, sector, role, seniority, life stage.
- **Situational** — a trigger event that puts them in-market right now (see the ICP section's
  "trigger" field in the Conversion Brief template): a recent failure, a new regulation, a
  budget cycle, a growth stage transition.
- **Psychographic** — what they already believe about the problem, which determines whether the
  page needs to build belief from zero or can start from "you already know this."

The test for whether a sub-market is narrow enough: can you write one sentence naming their
specific situation that a member of the sub-market would read and think "that's me, exactly,"
rather than "sure, that's broadly true"? If the sentence is broadly true of everyone, the
sub-market is still a category.

## Choosing between sub-markets when more than one is viable

Score each candidate against the four signals above, then check two things a scorecard misses:

**Existing proof.** A sub-market where the client already has customers, case studies or reviews is
cheaper to write for than one requiring proof to be built from nothing — Proof available in the
brief inherits directly from this choice.

**Fit with what's already built.** An offer, product or service built for one situation doesn't
automatically transfer to an adjacent one just because the pain looks similar; check that the
mechanism actually addresses this sub-market's specific version of the problem before committing
the brief to it.

Where two sub-markets score close, prefer the narrower one for the first brief. A narrow sub-market
that converts can be widened by running a second brief for the next segment; a broad brief that
underperforms is much harder to diagnose, because it's unclear which sub-audience inside it the
page failed.
