# Angles, names and the ladder

An angle changes the frame on an offer, not the offer. Same delivery, same price, same people —
different claim about what it is for and who is standing in front of it.

That makes angle work cheap, and it makes it a trap. **If a new angle appears to solve the problem,
check that the problem was framing.** Where the binding constraint from `value-equation.md` is
perceived likelihood or time delay, no amount of reframing touches it, and a sharper angle applied to
an offer that does not work produces faster disappointment rather than more sales. Angle work is for
offers that are sound and badly explained.

## Generate angles by variable, not by list

The usual approach is to run down a menu of lenses — outcome, time, pain, identity, and so on — and
produce a line for each. It works, and it produces a list nobody knows how to choose from.

Sort them by which of the four variables the angle puts in front instead. That ties the choice to the
diagnosis you already ran, and the shortlist writes itself: **lead with an angle that foregrounds the
binding constraint.**

**Foregrounding what the buyer ends up with**

- *The named result.* State the outcome concretely enough that the buyer could repeat it to someone
  else without your help. Specificity is the whole move — an outcome that could describe any
  competitor is not an angle.
- *The identity shift.* Frame the offer around who the buyer becomes, not what they receive. Strong
  where the purchase is discretionary and the buyer is choosing a version of themselves; weak where
  someone else signs the invoice.

**Foregrounding whether they'll believe it**

- *The mechanism.* Lead with how it works, not with what it produces. The most register-portable
  angle of the seven, because an explained method raises belief in every buying situation and offends
  none of them.
- *The narrow audience.* Address a smaller group by name. A claim made about a specific kind of
  buyer is more believable than the same claim made about everyone, and the reader in that group
  reads the narrowing as evidence you have seen his situation before.

**Foregrounding the wait**

- *The dated result.* Attach a timeframe to the outcome. Only usable where the date can actually be
  held; the first miss costs more than the angle ever earned, because the failure story travels.

**Foregrounding what it costs them**

- *The removed work.* Lead with what the buyer no longer has to do. Effective in proportion to how
  much the buyer dislikes the work, which is a question the brief's Voice of customer section
  answers and guesswork does not.

**Cutting across all four**

- *The contrarian frame.* Deny something the market believes, then support the denial. Highest
  ceiling and highest floor of the seven: it earns attention where it is true and defensible, and
  reads as posturing the moment it is neither.

Two variations per angle is enough to see whether it has anything in it. Mark the strongest two or
three and say what each is for — the one that works in a paid ad and the one that works in a
proposal are usually not the same line.

**Test an angle on the surface it was written for.** An angle written for cold traffic, tried on a
page reached by people who already know the brand, tells you nothing about the angle.

## Naming the offer

Giving the offer a name changes what it can do. Naming turns it from an instance of a category into a
particular thing, and a particular thing can be referred to by buyers among themselves, told apart
from the alternatives, and priced on its own terms rather than against the going rate for hours.
Corey Haines's `marketingskills` repo makes the same three-part case, in
`coreyhaines31/marketingskills`'s `offers/references/offer-anatomy.md`, and it is right.

Patterns that carry: named by the result, named by the method, named by who it is for, named by its
shape or duration. Patterns that don't: a generic description of the category, an internal tier code
the buyer cannot repeat, anything that pattern-matches to a get-rich pitch, and wordplay.

**The test, for B2B especially:** could the buyer's internal champion put this name in a calendar
invitation, or forward it to a colleague, without a paragraph of explanation attached? A name that
needs an explanatory sentence every time it appears is a name that will quietly stop being used, and
the offer reverts to being described as whatever category it belongs to — which is the thing the name
was supposed to prevent.

## The ladder, and when not to build one

An offer ladder is an entry rung, the main offer, and a premium rung, each leading to the next. It is
the right structure when the audience genuinely splits by budget or by readiness, and when you have
something to say to the ones who are not ready to buy the main thing yet.

Three rules keep it from becoming three offers that compete with each other:

1. **Each rung has to lead somewhere.** If the entry rung's buyer has no reason to move up once
   they've got what they paid for, it is not an entry rung — it is a cheaper product that cannibalises
   the main one.
2. **The steps have to be visible.** A buyer should be able to say what the next rung adds. Where the
   difference is only price and vague "more support," nobody upgrades.
3. **A ladder built before the core offer has proof is a ladder of unproven rungs.** This is the
   common failure and it is expensive: three untested offers take longer to learn from
   than one, and the learning is what the first year is actually for. Prove the core, then extend.

## When the answer is a different vehicle, not a different angle

Sometimes the diagnosis says the offer is aimed at a buyer who cannot buy it in this form — the
promise is too large for a self-serve product, or the work needs accountability nobody is providing,
or the seller has real delivery skill and no proof to sell a course on. That is a
`delivery-models.md` problem, and reframing it is rearranging the furniture. Say so plainly; a client
would rather be told the vehicle is wrong than be handed ten sharper ways to describe it.

## By register

The angle that opens one register closes another. Read only the row the brief named.

| Register | Angles that carry | Angles to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative B2B (DACH) | The mechanism and the narrow audience. Both raise belief without raising claim intensity, which is exactly the trade this reader wants. | The contrarian frame, which reads as picking a fight the buyer would then have to defend internally, and the identity shift, which misreads a purchase made on someone else's behalf. |
| Technical / industrial | The mechanism, decisively — this reader is buying an explanation. The narrow audience works when the narrowing is by application rather than by sector. | The identity shift, and any dated result stated without its operating conditions. |
| Consumer / lifestyle | The identity shift and the removed work. The named result works when it is picturable rather than measurable. | The mechanism at length, which is answering a question this reader did not ask. |
| Mission / nonprofit | The named result, stated as what the contribution accomplishes, and the mechanism, stated as how the work is done. | The contrarian frame against peer organisations, and any angle whose energy comes from urgency the reader will recognise as manufactured. |
| Direct-response | All seven are in play; the contrarian frame and the dated result do the most work at the top of the page. | Nothing structurally — but the dated result and the contrarian frame both have to survive being checked, and in this register they will be. |
