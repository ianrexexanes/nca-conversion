# Conversion psychology

Sixteen principles, kept from a much longer catalogue of mental models circulating in marketing
material — the fuller list runs past fifty once you count every named bias and technique separately.
Trimmed against one test: **does this principle change a specific decision in one of the eleven
sections**, not "is this a real, interesting phenomenon." A principle that's true but only ever
informs channel strategy, retention design, or a competing page-structure model doesn't earn a slot
here — this file is a tool for deciding what to write in a section, not a reading list.

Several entries below merge two or three source models that name the same underlying mechanism
differently. That's deliberate: a page-builder needs one decision per lever, not three labels for it.

Each entry names the mechanism, the section it's most actionable in, and how it's actually used
there — not the general phenomenon on its own.

## Jobs to Be Done

A buyer doesn't want the product; they want the job it does for them, and that job is nearly always a
couple of steps removed from whatever they literally typed into the inquiry form. **Problem, Outcome
and Solution** should be written against the job, not the product category — "a hole" the buyer is
hiring the drill to make, in the canonical phrasing. Concretely: if the Problem section describes a
feature gap rather than a consequence in the reader's day, the job hasn't been found yet.

## Loss Aversion / Prospect Theory

Behavioral-economics research puts the sting of a loss at roughly double the pleasure of an
equivalent gain, so avoiding a bad outcome pulls a reader harder than chasing an equally-sized good
one ever does. Useful in **Problem** (naming the cost of the status quo continuing, not just the
benefit of changing it), **Objections** (a resolved risk is worth more in the copy than an equivalent
promised gain), and **CTA** framing, used sparingly — leaning on it in every section reads as
manufactured pressure rather than an honest accounting of the stakes.

## Anchoring Effect

Whichever figure a reader meets first becomes the yardstick every number after it gets measured
against, which is why **Value stack** presents a comparison figure — a cost of inaction, a comparable
alternative's price, a pieced-together cost — before or alongside the number that matters, rather
than the price alone
with nothing to judge it against. Two closely related techniques fold in here rather than getting
their own entries: presenting a deliberately weaker option beside the target one so the target reads
as the obvious choice, and pre-selecting the tier you want chosen rather than leaving every option
visually equal — both are anchoring applied to a set of choices instead of a single number, and both
belong in the same Value stack decision this principle already governs.

## Contrast Effect

Things are judged relative to what they're placed beside, not in isolation. This is the specific
mechanism behind the **Problem-to-Outcome pairing**: a vividly stated "before" is what makes the
"after" in Outcome register as a real change rather than a generic improvement. Weak on either half
of the pair and the contrast — and the two sections' whole job — collapses.

## Framing Effect

Identical facts land differently depending on how they're stated — "90% success" against "10%
failure" describes the same number but not the same reader reaction. Governs phrasing in **Outcome**
and **Objections** specifically: frame within the belief the reader already holds about their
situation rather than against it. A related habit worth folding in here: don't open a section by
arguing the reader's existing view of their own problem is wrong before showing you understand it —
reframe what they already believe rather than contesting it, which is a framing decision as much as
it is a tone one.

## Price framing

Four related pricing-perception effects, treated as one decision for **Value stack**: a price ending
just under a round number reads as meaningfully cheaper than the round number itself, while a round
price reads as more premium and easier to process; a percentage discount feels larger below roughly
$100 and an absolute discount feels larger above it; a middle option in a three-tier set reads as the
reasonable choice specifically because a cheaper and a pricier option flank it; and the same figure
restated as a smaller recurring unit ("$3 a day" against "$90 a month") changes how expensive it
feels without changing what's owed. None of these four is a separate principle for a page-builder —
they're four levers on the same one decision, which number and which framing to put in front of the
reader, and mixing charm pricing with a premium positioning (or vice versa) undercuts whichever
register the rest of the page is arguing for.

## Social proof and authority

Two related trust shortcuts, usually deployed together in **Proof**: a reader trusts what similar
others have already chosen (counts, logos, review volume), and separately defers faster to a
credentialed source (named experts, certifications, "featured in" placements). They're distinct
mechanisms — one is about numerosity, the other about expertise — but both live in the same section
and both fail the same way when the count is unverifiable or the credential is stated too vaguely to
check; see `proof-library.md` for which artifact actually earns which register's trust.

## Availability Heuristic

A vivid, specific, easily-recalled example feels more probable than an abstract statistic, even when
the statistic is the stronger evidence. Governs a real trade-off inside **Proof**: one detailed,
name-and-number case study a reader can picture usually does more work than a larger but faceless
aggregate figure sitting beside it — use both where you have both, but don't assume the bigger number
is doing the persuading.

## Survivorship Bias

Publishing only the outcomes that worked, without saying whether they're typical, is the specific
integrity failure inside **Proof** that a strong-looking case study can quietly commit. Every result
shown should carry an implicit or explicit answer to "is this the best case or a typical one" — an
omission `register-profiles.md`'s Direct-response profile calls out by name as that register's most
damaging proof failure, precisely because that register's readers are the most practiced at spotting
it.

## Pratfall Effect

A credible source admitting a real, minor limitation is trusted more afterward, not less — perfection
reads as unlikely, and a stated limitation reads as candor. Directly actionable in **Objections**: a
plainly named "this isn't the right fit if X" line does more for trust than a page that implies the
offer is right for everyone, and it's the mechanism behind the small-verified-claim-beats-large-
unverifiable-one pattern `register-profiles.md` documents for Conservative B2B specifically.

## Curse of Knowledge

Expertise erases its own trail: a writer fluent in the subject stops being able to tell which parts
of an explanation actually need spelling out for someone who isn't. This is the specific failure mode
to check for while writing **Mechanism** — a term unremarkable inside the writer's own field can land
as opaque, or as padding, to a reader outside it — and the fix is testing the draft on someone
unfamiliar with the category, not just fact-checking it.

## Regret Aversion

People avoid choices that might cause future regret more than the expected outcome alone would
predict, which is the psychological case for **Guarantee** existing at all: an explicit, concrete
guarantee is what lets a reader act now instead of deferring the decision to avoid the possibility of
having chosen wrong.

## Reciprocity

Receiving something first creates a felt obligation to give something back. Does the most work on
**opt-in pages** (see `page-types.md`) — the free thing genuinely has to be valuable on its own terms
for this to function, not a token gesture that reads as a pretext for the email address.

## Commitment & Consistency

A small commitment makes a larger, consistent one more likely afterward. Relevant to **CTA**
sequencing on any page that asks for more than one thing in order — an email, then a call, then a
purchase — and to why a page's first ask should be sized to something the reader will actually
complete, rather than the biggest ask the seller wishes they'd make first.

## Status-Quo Bias

The current situation, however flawed, feels safer than a change, simply because it's already true.
This is the specific reason "keep doing nothing" is usually the real competitor named in the brief's
Competitive landscape section, and it's the objection **Objections** most often needs to resolve
first, ahead of any named competitor: not "why us over them" but "why change at all."

## Paradox of Choice

More options slow a decision down and increase the odds of no decision at all, rather than a better
one — the same mechanism sometimes cited under a different name for the time-to-decide version of the
effect. Governs **CTA** (one primary action, not three competing ones) and **Value stack** (three
tiers outperform seven, and a page recommending one clearly beats a page presenting all of them as
equally weighted).

## Why the rest were cut

Roughly forty further models were left out, by category rather than one at a time:

- **General business or growth strategy, not page copy.** First Principles, the Pareto Principle,
  Theory of Constraints, opportunity cost, diminishing returns, compounding, network effects, and
  most of the rest of the source's "Foundational Thinking" and "Growth & Scaling" categories help
  decide what to build or which channel to invest in. None of them changes a sentence inside a
  section of this page.
- **Channel frequency and multi-touch strategy, not single-page copy.** The mere-exposure effect and
  the "rule of seven" both argue for repeated exposure across touchpoints — real, but outside what a
  single page's copy controls.
- **Redundant restatements of a mechanism already kept.** Several source models rename an effect
  already covered above: a decoy option and a pre-selected default are both anchoring applied to a
  choice set; foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face are both commitment-and-consistency or anchoring
  wearing a different label; mimetic desire, the unity principle and simple liking/similarity all
  restate social proof's "others like me chose this" mechanism; and four separate behavior-design
  models (Fogg's Behavior Model, EAST, COM-B, and nudge theory generally) all reduce to the same
  motivation-ability-prompt triad under different acronyms, which is why none of the four gets its
  own entry once the underlying idea is covered.
- **A competing page-structure model.** The AIDA funnel is a legitimate structural frame, but this
  plugin already has one — the eleven sections in `page-architecture.md` — and naming a second,
  looser structural model alongside it invites the exact confusion about which one governs that this
  file exists to avoid.
- **Deliberately deferred, not dropped.** The scarcity and urgency heuristic is real and page-copy-
  relevant, but `register-profiles.md` already carries a full per-register treatment of exactly this
  lever — five archetypes, each with its own failure modes for stacked scarcity, fake urgency, and
  what counts as a legitimate deadline — in more depth than a general psychology note could add
  without duplicating it. Treat that dimension as this principle's real content.
- **Product and onboarding design, not page copy.** The endowment effect, the IKEA effect, the
  zero-price effect, the goal-gradient effect and the Zeigarnik effect mostly describe what happens
  after a purchase or signup, inside a product or an onboarding flow — outside what copy on this page
  can do anything about.
- **Diagnosis, not writing.** The fundamental attribution error is genuinely useful for figuring out
  *why* an existing page underperforms without blaming the visitor for it — that's `conversion-page-
  audit`'s job when it's investigating a failure, not a decision this skill makes while drafting new
  copy.
