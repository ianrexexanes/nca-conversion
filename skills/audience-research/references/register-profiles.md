# Register profiles

Five archetypes. One is selected per client, in the Conversion Brief, before any copy is written,
and every downstream skill inherits it unchanged.

`Conservative B2B (DACH)` · `Technical / industrial` · `Consumer / lifestyle` ·
`Mission / nonprofit` · `Direct-response`

## How register works

A conversion page has two independent layers, and the architecture of these skills rests entirely
on keeping them apart.

The first layer is the framework layer, and it is the reasoning engine. What a section has to
accomplish, in what order, against which objection — the value equation used as a diagnostic,
obstacle mapped to solution, objection mapped to belief shift mapped to proof, contrast and
anchoring around a price — is structural. It does not vary by audience. A conservative industrial
pricing page needs anchoring exactly as much as a consumer subscription page does; it just needs it
quiet. The frameworks travel everywhere, unchanged.

The second layer is register: how the page sounds, how hard it claims, what counts as proof,
whether urgency is usable at all, how directly it asks. This varies enormously, and it is not a
stylistic preference. Register is a claim about who is reading, and about what will make that
reader trust the page instead of closing it.

**Register is an input, never a default.** It is chosen once per client, written into the
Conversion Brief, and inherited by offer design, page copy, hooks, audit and editing without
re-deciding. A skill that picks its own register mid-project produces a page that is internally
inconsistent, and inconsistency is legible to a reader in a way that a wrong-but-consistent choice
is not. A page written entirely in the wrong register can at least be argued with. A page written
in two registers cannot be defended at all.

**`Direct-response` is one of five, and it is never the default.** This has to be said out loud,
because it is the register that gets applied by accident. It is the loudest of the five, the most
documented, the one most published copywriting material teaches, and the one a model reaches for
when nobody specifies. Applied to the audience it fits, it is extremely effective. Applied to the
wrong one it does not merely underperform — it does active damage. To a committee buyer in a
risk-averse sector, scarcity language is a fraud signal, and a page that reads as a scam does not
get a second look from anyone in the approval chain. The damage is invisible from inside the copy,
which is the whole problem: the page reads as energetic to whoever wrote it and as disqualifying to
whoever had to sign.

**The failure modes are the transferable half.** Each profile is described along the same seven
dimensions, and each dimension states what the register does and what getting it wrong looks like.
The description is the weaker half — plenty of bland pages satisfy every description here — and
the failure mode is what a real page can actually be tested against. A register you cannot fail is
not a register, it is a preference.

**Not every failure costs the same.** When a page misses its register on rhythm or formality alone,
that is an editing pass. When it misses on proof, scarcity handling and CTA at the same time, the
argument is wrong and no amount of rewriting fixes it, because those three encode what this
audience needs in order to say yes. Diagnose which kind of failure you have before rewriting
anything.

**Choosing one.** Read each profile's `Signals it's the right pick` subsection rather than its
character description. The signals are about the buying situation — how long the cycle runs, who
signs, what the sector's risk posture is, whether jargon is a trust signal or a barrier — and the
buying situation is what determines register. Where a client sits between two profiles, take the
more conservative of the two and borrow named dimensions from the other rather than blending
them; a page that averages two registers reads as neither. Where the audience is genuinely split —
an engineer and the procurement officer who signs, a practitioner and the executive he reports to —
that is usually not a register problem but a page-count problem, and two pages each held in one
register beat a single page trying to hold two. **`Mission / nonprofit` is the standing exception
to that.** Its audiences are split by definition, routing them to separate pages reads as
gatekeeping the people the organisation exists to serve, and its own `CTA directness` guidance is
to carry several participation paths on one page instead. Where a profile's guidance and this
general rule disagree, the profile wins.

## Conservative B2B (DACH)

Understated claims, proof before benefit, no urgency devices, consistent formal address,
credentials before promises. High-consideration purchases into a formal-address market where the
buyer's own credibility is on the line when he recommends you internally.

### Sentence rhythm

Short to medium declaratives, one fact each. Length varies with how much fact there is, not with
pacing. Paragraphs run two to four sentences and stop when the point has landed. A long sentence is
allowed when the qualification inside it is real; a long sentence built for cadence is not.
Fragments used for emphasis are rare and never appear twice in a stretch.

**Failure mode: staccato drift.** Three or four fragments in a row stops reading as emphasis and
starts reading as a pitch. To this buyer it signals a writer managing his own emotional state
rather than informing, and it sends the reader looking for what the rhythm is covering. The
opposite failure is just as real and quieter: six sentences carrying no new fact reads as padding,
gets skimmed, and takes the one real fact in the paragraph with it.

### Claim intensity

Claims sit at or slightly below what the evidence supports. Superlatives appear only when they are
countable and checkable. "The only provider in this market with three practising senior
practitioners" is permitted if it is true and verifiable; "the best provider in this market" is
not, because nothing can settle it. This register gains more from a small verified claim than from
a large unverifiable one, because the small one can be confirmed without asking anyone.

**Failure mode: one unbackable claim discredits the checkable ones beside it.** This reader does
not average claims, he audits them. A single "revolutionary" or "guaranteed results" next to five
verifiable facts causes him to re-read all five as marketing, so the cost of the bad claim is never
itself — it is everything adjacent to it. Under-claiming fails too, and fails quietly: if nothing is
asserted at all, the reader cannot work out what he is buying, and the page reads as a brochure
rather than an offer.

### Proof requirements

Every load-bearing claim needs an artefact the reader can inspect without contacting anyone. A
named person with a checkable role and employer. A number with a unit and a date. A document he can
download. A published price. Proof by adjective does not count in this register. Where proof does
not exist, the register requires saying so, because a stated absence is credible and a disguised
one is not.

**Failure mode: proof scoped to a different product.** The expensive failure is not missing proof,
it is proof that argues for the wrong purchase — client logos from a different service line,
unlabelled, so the reader concludes something false about what those companies actually bought. It
reads as evidence and functions as a counter-argument. Two more: testimonials without name, role
and company, which in this register are read as fabricated by default rather than merely as weak;
and claiming a proof artefact the business cannot yet produce, which turns a credibility asset into
a liability on the day someone asks for it. The shortest test is to take any three consecutive
claims on the page and ask, for each, what artefact the reader could inspect to check it without
contacting anyone. If fewer than two of the three have an answer, the page is out of register
however well it reads.

### Urgency and scarcity handling

Urgency comes from facts the seller does not control. The date something happens. A room that seats
a fixed number. An early rate with a published end date. A registration close. Each is verifiable
and none of them is a device. One scarcity axis is visible at a time, its reason is stated once,
and the reason is a property of the format rather than a lever on the reader.

**Failure mode: stacked scarcity inverts.** Two or more scarcity claims on one screen stop reading
as urgency and start reading as a seller who does not trust his own offer, and the effect gets
stronger as the price goes up. A scarcity number that later moves is worse: a count that changes in
public destroys the credibility of every number on the page, including the ones that were always
true. Countdown timers, live seat counters and "only three left" widgets fail here whether or not
they are accurate, because in this register the mechanism itself is the signal. The related trap is
the promise about the seller's own future conduct — "this will not be offered again" — which is the
one sentence in a scarcity block that the reader cannot check and the seller cannot be held to;
urgency from facts outside the seller's control is credible, urgency from the seller's resolve is a
device. Count the scarcity signals visible on one screen at the price: more than one and the page
is out of register no matter how many claims check out.

### CTA directness

One primary action, repeated in the same words every time it appears. The label names what happens
next rather than how the reader should feel. A lower-commitment second option sits beside it,
because a purchase needing internal sign-off often cannot be completed in one session — a
conversation, a document to forward, a callback. Under the button the mechanics are stated plainly:
what happens after submitting, when it happens, and the exact point at which the reader is
committed.

**Failure mode: an ambiguous commitment point.** If the reader cannot tell whether pressing the
button spends money, he does not press it, and he does not write in to ask. A label describing a
feeling gets parsed as evasion. Several competing primary actions mean that in a committee purchase
nobody knows what they are approving. The most common failure is quieter than all of these: exactly
one action and no lower-commitment alternative, which loses every reader who has to ask someone
else first — and that is most of them.

### Formality

One form of address, chosen once and held throughout. Full names and titles on first mention, with
the employer named wherever the employer is the credential. No contractions, no slang, and equally
no bureaucratic abstraction: formal in address, plain in vocabulary. Humour is available, but only
self-directed and only once.

**Failure mode: a single register break costs more than a whole page in the wrong register.** One
informal address on an otherwise formal page reads to a native speaker as an error rather than as
warmth, and it starts him wondering what else went unproofed. A consistently informal page is a
coherent choice that can be argued with; an inconsistent one cannot be defended at all. The second
failure is formality mistaken for abstraction, where every sentence is correctly formal and none of
them says anything a reader could check.

### Language notes

Written natively, not translated. A translated page carries source-language sentence structure that
a native reader detects without being able to name it, and detecting it is enough. Regional
orthography and punctuation are followed. Currency and number formats match local convention. Roles,
processes and qualifications are named the way this market names them rather than by their nearest
generic equivalent, and where a term of art has no local equivalent the register keeps the original
term rather than inventing a translation.

**Failure mode: translation tells cluster.** A slightly wrong idiom, a foreign sentence rhythm, a
thousands separator from another market, a job title that does not exist locally, an unstated VAT
treatment. Each is trivial alone; together they tell the reader the seller is not from here, which
in a conservative regional market is the one impression no other part of the page can recover. The
subtler failure is a page that is flawless in the target language but built on the source
language's argument — the sentences are native and the reasoning is foreign.

### Signals it's the right pick

Long sales cycles measured in weeks or months. Committee buying, or a single recommender who has to
carry the decision to someone else and be seen to have chosen well. A regulated or risk-averse
sector, or any market where a purchasing mistake is career-visible. A formal-address language
market. A price high enough that nobody signs it in one session alone. Credentials, certifications
and institutional membership carry more weight than outcome promises. And a decisive tell: if the
buyer would be embarrassed to forward the page to a colleague, the register is wrong regardless of
how the copy performs on its own.

The five dimensions that actually carry this register are scarcity handling, proof scoping,
formality consistency, CTA commitment clarity and translation tells. That weighting is the useful
part of the profile, because it tells you what a failure means. A page that misses only on rhythm
and claim intensity is fixable in an editing pass. A page that misses on those five has the wrong
argument, not the wrong prose, and rewriting sentences will not reach it.

## Technical / industrial

Specification-led, engineer to engineer, precision valued over persuasion. The reader is a
practitioner who will be held responsible for the selection, and who reads a page the way he reads
a datasheet.

### Sentence rhythm

Information-dense and tolerant of length. A long sentence is fine when every clause carries a
constraint, a condition or a tolerance. Tables, specification lists and diagrams outrank prose
wherever the content is genuinely tabular, and moving content out of paragraphs into a table is
usually an improvement rather than a compromise. Sentences are declarative and the subject is
usually the product or the process, not the reader.

**Failure mode: marketing rhythm reads as content-free.** Short punchy sentences, the pattern that
signals confidence in a consumer register, signal an absence of data here — the reader's immediate
question is what the sentence would say if it had a number in it. The mirror failure is length
without density: long sentences full of qualification that resolve to no measurable statement,
which reads as a vendor avoiding a commitment he knows he cannot meet.

### Claim intensity

Claims are quantified, with tolerances and operating conditions attached. A performance figure
without the conditions it was measured under is not a weaker claim, it is a different kind of
claim, and this reader treats the two as different categories. "Up to" is acceptable only when the
boundary condition is stated next to it. Where a figure is typical rather than guaranteed, say
which.

**Failure mode: the unqualified number.** A performance claim with no conditions invites the reader
to construct the worst-case interpretation and then disbelieve the whole page — he has done this
before, with vendors who turned out to be quoting a laboratory maximum. The second failure is
claiming a spec the product meets only in a configuration the page does not describe, which does
not surface at the copy stage and surfaces instead during qualification, where it costs the
relationship rather than the sale.

### Proof requirements

Datasheets, test standards cited by their correct designation, certification numbers, tolerance
ranges, third-party test reports, material specifications, and reference installations described by
application rather than by customer name. Where a standard governs the category, naming it and
stating conformance is worth more than any adjective. Drawings, dimensioned diagrams and
compatibility matrices are proof in this register, not decoration.

**Failure mode: social proof standing in where a specification was expected.** A testimonial in the
slot where a spec belongs reads as a substitution, and this reader knows what gets substituted for
data: bad data. Logos of well-known customers do not compensate, because he is not asking who
bought it, he is asking whether it fits his application. The related failure is proof that is real
but not comparable — a case study whose operating conditions differ from his in a way the page does
not disclose, which he will discover and treat as concealment.

### Urgency and scarcity handling

Essentially none. Timing in this market is driven by project schedules, tooling lead times,
qualification cycles and budget periods, none of which the seller controls or should pretend to.
The legitimate urgency signals are factual and logistical: a current lead time, a
last-time-buy notice, an end-of-life date, a scheduled standard revision. State them as
information, with the date they were accurate.

**Failure mode: any scarcity device disqualifies the vendor.** Countdown timers and limited-stock
badges read as consumer marketing applied to industrial procurement, and the conclusion the reader
draws is not that he should hurry but that this supplier is not one his organisation buys from. The
opposite failure is more common and costs more: failing to state lead times when lead time is the
actual constraint on the decision, which quietly loses the specification to whoever published
theirs.

### CTA directness

The request is for a technical artefact or a technical conversation: download the datasheet,
request a sample, upload a drawing for a quotation, book a call with an application engineer, check
compatibility. Multiple entry points are correct here because the buyer arrives at different depths
— someone comparing options needs the datasheet, someone specifying needs the engineer. Say which
is which.

**Failure mode: a generic conversion CTA where the buyer wanted a document.** "Get started" in the
slot where a datasheet link belongs sends the engineer to a competitor's ungated one, and that loss
never registers as a loss because he never identified himself. The related failure is gating the
datasheet behind a form at the evaluation stage: it converts a small number of people who were
buying anyway and removes the page from consideration by everyone still deciding. Gate the
application support, not the specification.

### Formality

Professional, unadorned and sparse. Jargon is used correctly and in full, because the terminology
is a competence test — the reader is checking whether you work in his field before he checks
whether your product fits. Abbreviations are given in the form the industry uses. Adjectives are
rationed; the noun and the number do the work.

**Failure mode: getting a term of art slightly wrong.** A misused standard designation, a unit
applied to the wrong quantity, or a process named the way an outsider names it tells the reader you
are not from this field, and no amount of correct content afterwards recovers it. The opposite
failure is patronising: explaining a term this reader uses every day inserts distance where none
existed and signals that the page was written for someone else.

### Language notes

Unit conventions, standards bodies and part-numbering follow the industry rather than the marketing
department. State which unit system is authoritative when both appear, and give conversions rather
than substitutions. Use the industry's own names for processes, materials and roles. Where content
is localised, technical terms usually should not be translated at all, because the original term is
what the reader searches for and what appears in his own documentation.

**Failure mode: mixed or unstated unit conventions.** A page carrying both metric and imperial
figures without saying which is nominal and which is converted forces the reader to verify
everything or trust nothing, and he will not verify. The subtler failure is a localised page whose
technical vocabulary has been translated into generic equivalents, which breaks the reader's search
terms, breaks the match with his internal documentation, and makes a precise product look
approximate.

### Signals it's the right pick

The buyer is a practitioner who will personally answer for the choice. There is a specification, a
drawing, a tolerance or a load case in the conversation. The purchase enters a qualification,
approval or homologation process before money moves. The technical evaluator and the purchasing
authority are different people with different questions. Competitors publish datasheets and your
client is being compared against them line by line. And the clearest signal of all: jargon is a
trust signal rather than a barrier — if using the correct term makes the page more credible instead
of less accessible, this is the register.

The load-bearing dimensions here are claim intensity, proof requirements, CTA directness and
terminology — the last of which lives in `Formality` and `Language notes` but behaves like a
structural failure rather than a stylistic one, because a single misused term of art is a
competence signal and no correct sentence after it recovers the reader. A page failing on those has
the wrong argument: an unqualified figure, a testimonial standing where a datasheet belongs, or a
specification gated behind a form are each a sufficient reason for this buyer to leave, and none of
them is reachable by rewriting prose. Sentence rhythm and tone are the editing-pass pair. Urgency
is the odd one out and worth checking separately, because its failure is binary rather than graded
— one scarcity device on the page is disqualifying, and one deletion fixes it.

## Consumer / lifestyle

Warm, sensory, emotionally direct, low jargon. An individual spending their own discretionary money
on an experience or an object, deciding largely alone and largely quickly.

### Sentence rhythm

Shorter and more varied than the B2B registers, with cadence doing real work. This is one of the
few registers where rhythm is legitimately part of the argument, because part of what is being sold
is how the thing feels, and the sentence shape carries that. Second person throughout. Concrete
sensory nouns rather than abstractions, and present tense wherever the reader can be placed inside
the experience.

**Failure mode: rhythm with nothing under it.** Cadence without a specific image is advertising
copy that nobody finishes reading, and this audience has an unusually high tolerance for leaving.
The opposite failure is over-explanation: long, careful, feature-complete sentences that answer
questions the reader had not asked, which flattens the feeling the page exists to create and turns
a discretionary purchase into an evaluation the reader would rather not conduct.

### Claim intensity

Emotionally direct, but the claims that actually land are experiential and specific rather than
superlative — what the reader will be doing, seeing or holding, and when. "You are in the water by
nine" beats "an unforgettable experience" not because it claims less but because it is an image the
reader can put himself inside. Specificity is the intensity.

**Failure mode: superlatives occupy the space a specific image would fill.** "Best", "amazing" and
"unforgettable" are not disbelieved so much as unread; the cost is the sentence they displaced. The
more expensive failure is inflating the claim past what the experience delivers, which converts
well and reviews badly — and where reviews carry a large share of acquisition, an over-promising
page can keep damaging it long after the copy is fixed. How large that share is varies by category;
check the client's own review volume rather than assuming it.

### Proof requirements

Photography of the actual thing, in the actual place, showing the actual people. Reviews with
volume, recency and a named platform. Ratings the reader can go and verify. Video where the
experience is hard to imagine. The common finding is that user-generated images outperform
commissioned ones for exactly the reason they look worse — worth testing rather than assuming,
since it inverts where production quality is itself part of the product. Named locations, real
timings, honest weather.

**Failure mode: stock photography that reads as stock.** This reader identifies it instantly and
concludes the real thing is worse than the picture — a conclusion no copy on the page can reverse.
A curated wall of five-star quotes fails similarly: the absence of any three-star review is itself
read as filtering, and the common finding is that a review set with visible mixed opinions
outperforms a uniformly perfect one. Treat that as a hypothesis to test on the client's own
traffic, not as a finding to hand them. Reviews without a source platform are treated as invented.

### Urgency and scarcity handling

Legitimate and expected here, provided it is real. Seasonal availability, booking windows, actual
remaining stock, a departure date, a last service of the day. This audience is accustomed to
scarcity in this category and does not read it as manipulation when it corresponds to something
true. State the mechanism briefly rather than decorating it.

**Failure mode: scarcity the reader can falsify in one click.** A countdown that resets on reload,
a "three left" badge contradicted by the booking engine on the next page, or a sale that has been
ending for a month — each is recognised widely enough now that you should plan on this reader
spotting it, and each can turn a warm page into a suspicious one. The other failure is permanent
urgency: a page in constant promotion trains its own audience to wait, which is the opposite of the
intended effect and is invisible in a single campaign's numbers.

### CTA directness

One obvious action, low friction, repeated as the reader scrolls, with the price visible before the
click. Book, buy, reserve, join. The number of fields between intent and confirmation usually
carries a conversion cost, and it is one of the few things on the page cheap enough to measure
directly rather than argue about — so measure it, and until you have, ask for the minimum the
transaction actually requires and collect the rest afterwards.

**Failure mode: friction where the decision was already made.** A form standing where a button
belonged loses the reader who had decided, and those are the most valuable readers on the page. The
second failure is a hidden price: in discretionary spend an unstated price commonly reads as
expensive, the reader leaves without asking, and the page records it as ordinary non-conversion
rather than as the specific objection it was. That is the default hypothesis for a hidden-price
page, not a measured fact about this one.

### Formality

Informal, direct, contractions natural, first and second person. The brand sounds like a person who
does the thing it sells. Humour is available. Sentences may begin with conjunctions. Politeness
matters more than correctness, and warmth matters more than either.

**Failure mode: the corporate voice in a personal category.** Institutional phrasing on a page
selling an experience reads as a company that outsources the thing it is selling, which is often
literally what the reader concludes. The mirror failure is worse because it is legible as an
attempt: borrowed slang or a youth voice the brand has not earned, which the target audience
identifies faster than anyone and treats as condescension.

### Language notes

Use the words customers use, taken from reviews, search queries and support tickets rather than
from the internal product taxonomy. Local idiom is an asset here in a way it is not elsewhere.
Where a page is localised, it needs to be rewritten in the target language rather than translated,
because the emotional content is carried by idiom and idiom does not survive translation.

**Failure mode: internal naming leaking onto the page.** A product named the way the operations
team names it makes the reader work, and this reader will not. The localisation failure is
distinctive: a machine-translated experiential page can be grammatically flawless and emotionally
dead, so it passes every review a non-native reader can perform and fails with every native reader
who lands on it.

### Signals it's the right pick

One person spending their own money, without needing anyone's approval. Short consideration —
minutes to days, not weeks. The experience or the object is the product, and the purchase is partly
emotional and legitimately allowed to be. Price is low enough to decide alone and usually visible
on the page. Reviews and photography, rather than credentials, are the trust currency. Competitors
sell on feeling rather than on specification. If the reader would describe the purchase to a friend
in terms of how it felt rather than what it did, this is the register.

The load-bearing dimensions are proof requirements, CTA directness and urgency handling, and the
distinctive thing about this register is that two of the three are not copy at all. Stock
photography, unsourced reviews, a form standing where a button belongs and a price hidden until
checkout are asset and flow problems, so a page here that reads well and converts badly is usually
failing outside the words, and a copy rewrite will not reach it — diagnose before rewriting.
Sentence rhythm, formality and vocabulary are the editing pass. Claim intensity sits between the
two: inflating past what the experience delivers reads fine on the page and fails later in the
reviews, which makes it the one failure whose cost does not appear in the conversion rate you are
looking at.

## Mission / nonprofit

Dignity first, never exploiting the condition the organisation exists to relieve, no commercial
pressure. Usually more than one audience on the same page, with different needs and different
levels of vulnerability.

### Sentence rhythm

Plain, calm and unhurried, with space around statements. Sentences name a subject and a verb and
avoid the institutional passive. Short paragraphs, but short because the thought is complete rather
than because the page is pushing. The reader is given room to arrive at a conclusion rather than
being marched to one.

**Failure mode: urgency rhythm applied to a cause.** Stacked short fragments make an organisation
read as a campaign and a beneficiary read as a case, and the people most sensitive to that
distinction are the beneficiaries themselves. The opposite failure is institutional prose with no
agent in it — sentences where things are done, supported and facilitated by nobody — which reads as
an organisation protecting itself rather than serving anyone.

### Claim intensity

Understated. State what the organisation does, at the scale it actually does it, in the period it
did it. Attribution matters more here than magnitude: say what the organisation contributed rather
than what happened in the world around it. Where an outcome had many causes, name them.

**Failure mode: overstated impact is the single most damaging error available in this register.**
It is checkable by exactly the people whose trust is the asset — funders, partners, the community
served, and increasingly a journalist. Inflated need statistics are the specific version of this,
because they are usually borrowed from a secondary source, easy to trace, and frequently out of
date; a wrong figure about the problem casts doubt on every figure about the response. Under-
claiming has a real cost too, and it is the failure well-run organisations actually make: doing
substantial work and describing it so modestly that no funder can tell it apart from a dormant one.

### Proof requirements

Audited accounts, the split between programme and administration, governance and board
composition, named partners and funders, and at least one outcome traceable end to end from input
to result. Both kinds of proof are needed: an individual account that shows what the work looks
like, and a figure with a denominator that shows how much of it there is.

**Failure mode: the wrong kind of proof for the audience reading.** An emotional account standing
where a funder needed accountability data reads as deflection, and the funder does not ask, he
declines. The reverse is equally common: numbers with no human referent, which satisfy a compliance
requirement and move nobody to act. The third failure is proof that uses a person's story without
their evident consent or dignity, which can be effective in the short term and is a reputational
liability the moment anyone in the community sees it.

### Urgency and scarcity handling

Real deadlines exist and are usable: a matching-gift window that genuinely closes, a season, a
campaign end, a grant deadline, a service that runs on fixed dates. State the deadline and the
reason. Nothing else.

**Failure mode: manufactured urgency here is not merely ineffective, it is a dignity violation.**
It monetises the condition the organisation exists to relieve, and it is the failure a board
notices first and a beneficiary notices worst. The second failure is perpetual emergency framing —
every communication an urgent appeal — which exhausts a donor base, trains supporters to ignore the
next one, and makes a genuine crisis unannounceable when one arrives.

### CTA directness

Clear and specific about what happens to the money or the time, with several participation levels
because the audiences differ: donate, volunteer, refer someone, request support, subscribe. Each
audience needs its own visible path, and the paths must not compete for the same slot. Say what a
given amount actually does, with the same specificity you would use for a price.

**Failure mode: one loud donate button serving every audience at once.** The signature failure of
this register is the page that asks a person who came looking for help to give money — it is
invisible to whoever built the page and unmistakable to the person it happens to. The second
failure is a vague ask: the common finding is that "support our work" converts worse than a stated
amount tied to a stated outcome, and a vague ask also makes the organisation sound unsure what it
needs.

### Formality

Respectful, person-first, plain. The organisation is a participant rather than a saviour, and the
people it serves are people rather than conditions or categories. Formality level follows the
audience — a funder page and a support page can differ — but the dignity standard does not move
between them. No pity, no heroism, no rescue framing.

**Failure mode: language that describes people by their condition.** It is visible immediately to
the community served and invisible to the person who wrote it, which is what makes it persistent.
The related failure is saviour framing, where the organisation is the subject of every sentence
about work done for other people; it reads well to a certain kind of donor and badly to everyone
whose trust the organisation actually depends on.

### Language notes

Use the terms the community uses about itself, and verify them with the community rather than with
a style guide. Where a condition, group or identity has a contested name, the contest is itself
information, and the choice should be made deliberately and documented. Where the organisation
operates across languages, terminology must be checked in each of them separately, because the
preferred term in one language is frequently not the translation of the preferred term in another.

**Failure mode: a term the community retired years ago, still on the page.** It tells every
informed reader that nothing else on the page is current either, and it is the fastest available
way to lose the confidence of the exact audience the organisation exists for. The subtler failure
is clinical vocabulary imported wholesale from a medical or legal source, which is accurate,
defensible, and reads to the person living with it as being described rather than addressed.

### Signals it's the right pick

Two or more audiences with materially different needs read the same page — beneficiary, funder,
volunteer, referrer. The organisation's legitimacy is its principal asset, and it can be damaged
faster than it can be built. A board, a regulator or an institutional funder reads the page. The
cause is larger than the organisation and the copy has to keep that order. Any commercial pressure
on the page would be a reputational cost rather than a conversion gain. And a clarifying test: if
using a persuasion technique that works elsewhere would embarrass the organisation in front of the
people it serves, this is the register.

This register has the fewest editing-pass failures of the five, because most of its failure modes
are decisions rather than sentences. Overstated impact, manufactured urgency, saviour framing, a
term the community has retired, and one donate button serving every audience at once are all
choices about what the organisation is willing to do, and every one of them is load-bearing.
Sentence rhythm is the only dimension where a miss is genuinely a rewrite. That has a practical
consequence worth stating plainly: a page failing on the dignity dimensions cannot be signed off by
a copywriter or a client contact alone — it needs someone from the community the organisation
serves to read it, and where that review is not available, the honest position is that the page has
not been checked rather than that it passed.

## Direct-response

Outcome forward, specific numbers, hard CTA, explicit risk reversal. A self-serve purchase decided
in one session by one person who is fluent in this register and reading it as native rather than as
pressure.

### Sentence rhythm

Short declaratives, deliberately shorter than natural speech. One idea per line. Fragments used as
punctuation. The rhythm is a tool here and it is used openly — the page does not pretend to be
neutral information, and pretending would cost it more than the directness does. Questions asked
and then answered in the same breath to keep momentum.

**Failure mode: cadence sustained past the point where it carries content.** Held for a full page,
the pattern stops reading as confidence and starts reading as a pitch, and the reader begins
skimming for the price. The register-specific failure is borrowed rhythm: this cadence is strongly
associated with particular writers, and a page that reproduces it without the numbers underneath
reads as an imitation of a marketer rather than as an operator who knows his own business — which
is the exact opposite of the credibility the rhythm is supposed to buy.

### Claim intensity

High, but the discipline that makes it work is that the claim is a number. Round, specific, stated
plainly, and attached to a period. The intensity comes from the specificity, not from adjectives —
this register's actual native sentence is arithmetic said out loud, not a superlative. State the
input, the output and the interval.

**Failure mode: the confidence copied and the arithmetic dropped.** Most attempts at this register
reproduce the volume and omit the maths, producing loud unquantified claims — the worst available
combination, carrying direct-response's risk profile with none of its evidence. The second failure
is a number the business cannot defend under scrutiny, which matters more here than in any other
register, because this audience is composed of other operators who will run the figures and publish
the discrepancy.

### Proof requirements

Numbers with a mechanism attached. Account screenshots with the date range visible, before-and-
after with the period named, a stated method the reader could run himself, denominators alongside
percentages, and results from people who are not the seller. The strongest proof in this register
is a walkthrough of how the outcome was produced, because it is the one form a competitor cannot
fabricate cheaply.

**Failure mode: unfalsifiable proof.** Revenue screenshots with no timeframe, results with no
denominator, testimonials with no verifiable person. This audience is unusually literate in fake
proof — it is the register with the most fraud in it, so its readers have the most practice — which
means a weak proof asset here is more damaging than no proof asset at all. The related failure is
survivorship: publishing the best outcome as though it were the typical one, without saying which
it is.

### Urgency and scarcity handling

Explicit and structural. A real deadline, a cohort cap, a price that steps on a published date, a
bonus that expires. This is the one register where scarcity is a primary mechanism rather than a
seasoning, and where the reader expects it and is not alienated by it — provided it holds.

**Failure mode: scarcity that resets.** A deadline that passes and moves is this register's most
common self-inflicted wound, because the audience checks, screenshots and shares it, and the
resulting credibility loss attaches to the seller rather than the campaign. Scarcity is
simultaneously this register's most powerful tool and its most public liability, and the
asymmetry matters: one honoured deadline buys very little, one broken one costs a great deal.
Stacking is a lesser but real failure here too — three scarcity mechanisms on one page dilute each
other even in an audience that accepts one.

### CTA directness

One action, commanded rather than suggested. The button says what the reader is doing and the copy
around it says what happens next, at what price, on what terms. Risk reversal is stated explicitly,
with its conditions and its duration — a guarantee whose terms are visible is part of the offer
architecture, not a legal footnote.

**Failure mode: pressure without the structure that makes pressure fair.** A hard CTA with no risk
reversal is this register at its worst, and it is what gives the register its reputation. The
mirror failure is a guarantee with vague conditions, which resolves in one of two ways and both are
bad: unhonoured, which becomes public in a market that talks; or exploited, which quietly makes the
offer unviable. Write the guarantee's conditions as though you expect them to be tested, because
they will be.

### Formality

Informal, first person, direct address, contractions throughout. Blunt about what is not working
and specific about what to do instead. Hedging verbs are removed rather than softened, and the page
does not flatter the reader or announce its own method before executing it.

**Failure mode: bluntness that curdles into contempt.** Directness works because it is paired with
warmth; without the warmth it reads as a seller who thinks poorly of his buyer, and the buyer
notices. The other failure is cosplay: importing a specific person's catchphrases wholesale, which
produces a page in someone else's voice and is legible as such. Take the structure — short lines,
numbers spoken plainly, diagnosis before prescription, no throat-clearing — and write it in the
client's own words.

### Language notes

Plain words, no category jargon, no abstraction. Numbers written the way they would be said aloud.
Verbs in the active voice, and the reader as the subject of the sentences describing outcomes.
Where the audience has its own vocabulary for money and results, use it, and use it accurately.

**Failure mode: regulated claims, which this register's natural sentence walks straight into.**
Earnings claims, health outcomes, financial returns and business-opportunity results are governed
in most markets, and the register's default construction — a specific outcome attached to a
timeframe and implied as typical — is precisely the construction the rules address. Check the claim
surface before writing, not after. The second, smaller failure is hedging language creeping back in
during review, which does not make a regulated claim compliant and does neutralise the register.

### Signals it's the right pick

A self-serve purchase one person completes in a single session, with no committee, no procurement
and no approval chain. Short consideration. The seller's own brand rather than a conservative
client's. A measurable claim that survives audit, with the underlying data available. An audience
already fluent in this register, so the page reads as native rather than as pressure. No regulated
claim surface, or one you have checked. And the disqualifying test, which is the important half:
if the buyer will forward this page to anyone who has to approve the spend, do not use this
register — it is optimised for a reader who can say yes alone, and it actively damages the page in
front of anyone who cannot.

This register has the fewest safe failures of the five. Claim intensity, proof requirements,
urgency handling and CTA risk reversal are all load-bearing, and `Language notes` carries a fifth
that is not a conversion problem at all: a regulated claim is a legal exposure, and it does not
become acceptable by converting well. Sentence rhythm and formality are nominally the editing pass,
with one caveat worth more than the rest of this paragraph — borrowed cadence is usually a symptom,
not a style problem. A page reaches for someone else's rhythm when the arithmetic underneath is not
there, so treat copy that sounds like a famous marketer as a claim-intensity diagnosis first and a
prose one second.
