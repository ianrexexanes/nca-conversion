# Headline formulas

Sentence constructions, grouped by which slot carries the weight. Slots are written `‹like this›`.

## The boundary: this file decides the sentence

**This file decides the sentence. `hook-taxonomy.md` decides the claim.**

Almost any construction below can be filled with almost any of the eight hook types, which is
exactly why they are two files: choosing an angle and choosing a shape are separate decisions, and
collapsing them produces a set of variants that are all the same angle in different grammar.

Every construction below carries a **Suits** and a **Never**, and **both are slot claims, not angle
claims.** A slot claim is about grammar: whether this shape has room for what a register needs the
line to carry, and whether it seats the right party in the subject position. It asks whether an
imperative reads as instruction or as command, whether a colon reads as precision or as presumption,
whether a number in the subject slot reads as evidence or as noise.

The taxonomy asks a different question — whether this register accepts the *promise* being made at
all. Do not read a **Suits** here as the taxonomy's blessing on an angle: a construction can suit a
register perfectly and still be the wrong shape to pour a forbidden claim into. When a construction
is refused below and the angle is refused there, those are two independent refusals and both apply;
when this file suits a register and the taxonomy substitutes the type, the taxonomy wins.

## Surfaces bind before register does

The surface is a hard limit. It is decided first because no register makes a line fit a button.

| Surface | The limit | What it rules out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hero line** | Two lines at a phone width, read with the subhead and button visible | Nothing structurally — this is the only surface where a construction may be incomplete on its own, because the subhead finishes it |
| **Email subject** | Design for roughly the first thirty-five characters; assume the rest is not seen | Any shape whose payoff sits after a colon, a dash or a comma. Front-load or lose it |
| **Ad first line** | Must work with the sound off and against a thumb that has already decided | Shapes needing two terms held in mind at once, unless both are visible in one glance |
| **Social opener** | Truncates at the platform's *see more* boundary | Shapes that give away the whole claim before the cut, and shapes that give away nothing |
| **Button label** | Two to five words, imperative | Everything below except group G |

A construction that survives the surface and then fails the register is a normal outcome; try
another construction for the same type before changing the type.

## A. The result is the object

The reader or the product is the subject; the result is what gets done.

**`‹verb› ‹the reader's thing› ‹to a stated end state›`**
> *Close the audit file before the notified body opens it.*
**Fill rule:** the end state has to be recognisable without the product being explained.
**Suits** `Technical / industrial` and `Conservative B2B (DACH)` — the imperative governs a task, not the reader's will, so it lands as instruction rather than command. That is the only imperative these two accept outside a button.
**Never** as a button label — the shape is too long once the end state is specific enough to matter.

**`‹end state›, ‹the condition that makes it checkable›`**
> *No documentation findings, across the audits prepared last year.*
**Fill rule:** the second slot is evidence, not emphasis. If it can be deleted without weakening the
claim, it was decoration.
**Suits** `Conservative B2B (DACH)` — the comma clause is a dedicated grammatical slot for evidence, so proof is structural here rather than bolted on. This is the register that does not read the claim until the evidence is inside the same sentence.
**Never** as an email subject — the checkable half sits after the comma, which is where the
truncation lands, leaving a bare superlative.

**`‹input› becomes ‹output›`**
> *A drawing on Monday is a commissioned line six weeks later.*
**Fill rule:** both slots must be things the reader already handles. A transformation between two
abstractions is a tagline.
**Suits** `Technical / industrial` — both slots take concrete nouns and the verb carries no intensity of its own, so every bit of force in the sentence comes from the two nouns. That is the only place this register lets force come from.
**Never** in `Mission / nonprofit` when the input slot holds a person — the shape's subject position
is the problem there, not its tone, and no rewording rescues it.

## B. The line is defined by an absence

**`‹result› without ‹the specific cost usually paid›`**
> *Ship a compliant technical file without adding a documentation hire.*
**Fill rule:** the second slot names a cost the reader is paying *now*, in his words. Generic
difficulty in that slot converts the whole line into a category description.
**Suits** `Direct-response` and `Consumer / lifestyle` — the result is front-loaded and the qualification is deferred to a prepositional tail, which matches the order both registers scan in; a skimmer who drops the tail still has the claim.
**Never** in `Technical / industrial` as written — the shape has no slot for the operating condition,
and an unqualified capability claim is read here as a laboratory maximum. Add the condition and it
becomes construction E instead.

**`No ‹thing the reader expects to have to do›. ‹what happens instead›.`**
> *No new operator training. The interface is the one already on the line.*
**Fill rule:** the first sentence must be a thing the reader was actively dreading, otherwise the
second sentence answers a question nobody asked.
**Suits** `Technical / industrial` — two clipped declaratives with no connective between them, which is this register's own sentence shape. The full stop does the work a *because* would do elsewhere, and this reader supplies the causal link himself.
**Never** as a social opener — the negation gives the whole claim away before the cut, so nothing is
left to expand.

**`‹audience› do not ‹the thing everyone assumes is required›.`**
> *Our clients do not keep a documentation specialist on staff.*
**Fill rule:** the assumption has to be widely enough held that contradicting it is news. Check it
against how competitors describe the category, not against your own view of it.
**Suits** `Conservative B2B (DACH)` — the subject slot holds a third party rather than the reader, so a contradiction arrives as an observation about a population instead of a correction aimed at him. Third-person subject is what lets this register carry a contradiction politely.
**Never** in `Consumer / lifestyle` — the shape asserts something about a group the reader has not
yet joined, and this register's reader will not do the work of placing himself in it.

## C. The line names the reader

**`For ‹role› at ‹situation›.`**
> *For plant engineers running a coating line on two shifts.*
**Fill rule:** the situation slot does the excluding. A role with no situation attached excludes
nobody and has spent the line saying hello.
**Suits** `Conservative B2B (DACH)` and `Technical / industrial` — a verbless fragment with no addressee pronoun, so it scopes without addressing. Both registers accept being described to and resist being spoken to.
**Never** in `Direct-response` on its own — the shape has no slot for the disqualifier that register
expects, so it reads as an opening rather than a filter.

**`If ‹the reader's exact current state›, ‹what this is›.`**
> *If you have already paid for a benefits appointment you could not get to, start here.*
**Fill rule:** the state has to be one the reader would recognise in himself without being flattered
or accused.
**Suits** `Mission / nonprofit` and `Consumer / lifestyle` — the conditional seats the reader in the protasis, where he can decline the description without being contradicted. That is the only grammar the Mission register permits for addressing someone directly.
**Never** in `Conservative B2B (DACH)` when the state slot holds a mistake — the shape hands the
reader ownership of a failure, and not owning one is most of why he is buying at all.

**`‹audience›: ‹the claim›`**
> *Freelance designers billing project by project: the retainer conversation is one email.*
**Fill rule:** the audience label has to be one the reader uses about himself, not one the market
uses about him.
**Suits** `Direct-response` — the colon asserts the audience label as settled before the claim is made. Only where an exclusionary label is a welcome signal does that presumption read as confidence rather than as being sorted by a stranger.
**Never** as an email subject — the payoff is behind the colon and the colon is roughly where the
line gets cut.

## D. The line only means something against a named alternative

**`Built for ‹the case the category handles badly›.`**
> *Built for thin-wall tube under 3 mm, which line-scan systems average away.*
**Fill rule:** the badly-handled case has to be one the reader has personally been bitten by. A
weakness he has not met is an argument with nobody.
**Suits** `Technical / industrial` — the past participle takes an application as its object, so the shape has a slot for a condition and none for an adjective. That is the register's whole preference expressed as grammar.
**Never** as a button label or a subject line — comparison needs both terms present, and neither
surface holds two.

**`‹the common approach› ‹fails in a named condition›. ‹what happens instead›.`**
> *Most audit prep starts with the technical file. The findings start in change control.*
**Fill rule:** the named condition must be the reader's normal condition, not an edge case.
**Suits** `Conservative B2B (DACH)` — the contradicted party occupies the subject slot of the first sentence and it is not the reader, which is this register's grammatical precondition for accepting a contradiction at all.
**Never** in `Mission / nonprofit` when the common approach belongs to a peer organisation —
the shape makes the sector the antagonist, and the funder reading it works in that sector.

**`Before ‹state, with a figure›. After ‹state, same figure›.`**
> *Nine suppliers unaudited at the start of the year. Two at the end, both scheduled.*
**Fill rule:** the same quantity on both ends, measured the same way. Two different measures in the
two slots is the shape's characteristic lie.
**Suits** `Direct-response` and `Technical / industrial` — two parallel fragments hold two values in the same syntactic position, which is what makes them comparable at a glance. Fill both slots with numbers and the shape reads as measurement; fill them with adjectives and the same shape reads as boasting.
**Never** in `Mission / nonprofit` with a person in either slot, and **never** in
`Consumer / lifestyle` at all — the shape asks the reader to evaluate, and evaluation is the mode
this register's purchase is trying to avoid.

## E. The weight is a number

**`‹figure›, measured at ‹condition›.`**
> *0.05 mm repeatability, at 40 °C ambient, over an eight-hour shift.*
**Fill rule:** the condition slot is not optional and not a footnote. Without it the shape is
construction A wearing a number.
**Suits** `Technical / industrial` — the appositive after the comma is a dedicated slot for the operating window, and no other construction in this file has one. That is why a specification-reading register defaults to this shape.
**Never** in `Consumer / lifestyle` — the shape's payload is precision, and precision here displaces
the image that was doing the selling.

**`‹number› ‹unit› ‹over a stated period›, ‹denominator›.`**
> *212 volunteer shifts covered last quarter, out of 240 rostered.*
**Fill rule:** the denominator is what separates this from a boast. If it is unknown, the shape is
unavailable — say `NOT ESTABLISHED` rather than dropping the slot.
**Suits** `Mission / nonprofit` — the shape's fourth slot is a denominator, and this is the only register that reads a figure without one as incomplete rather than merely unimpressive.
**Never** as an ad first line — four slots is more than a scrolling reader will hold, and the
denominator, which is the honest half, is the one that gets dropped.

## F. The line is a question

**`‹the exact question the reader would type, or ask a colleague›?`**
> *Do we have to re-validate the whole file, or only the changed sections?*
**Fill rule:** take it verbatim from search queries, support tickets or sales calls. A question
written by the seller reads as rhetorical, and rhetorical questions get answered *no*.
**Suits** `Consumer / lifestyle` and `Direct-response` — an interrogative hands the reader the next turn, which both can afford because the answer is one scroll away. A register whose reader forwards the page cannot: the question travels and the answer does not.
**Never** as a button label, and **never** in `Technical / industrial` when the answer is a
specification the reader expected the page to simply state — asking it back reads as withholding.

**`Which of these is the problem: ‹a›, ‹b›, or ‹c›?`**
> *Is it the roster, the van, or the referrals?*
**Fill rule:** three options, mutually exclusive, all real. A list with an obvious intended answer is
a rhetorical question with extra steps.
**Suits** `Conservative B2B (DACH)` and `Mission / nonprofit` — a closed list lets a reader self-sort without being told which one he is. Mission needs precisely that, because several audiences read the same page and none may be assigned.
**Never** as a subject line or an ad first line — the shape needs all three options visible, and
neither surface shows them.

## G. Imperatives, and what a button may say

Only this group is available on a button.

**`‹verb› ‹the exact artefact›`**
> *Download the gap-analysis checklist.*
**Fill rule:** name the thing, not the category of thing. *Get the guide* and *Download the
gap-analysis checklist* are the same length and not the same promise.
**Suits** `Technical / industrial` — the object slot takes a document, and a named document is the
only button in this register that does not ask the reader to identify himself before he is ready.
**Never** in `Direct-response` as the primary button — the object slot holds a document rather than
a transaction, and a document button there defers the very thing the page has spent its length
setting up.

**`‹verb› ‹the transaction, with its terms in the words beside it›`**
> *Book the 30-minute review.* — with the terms stated under the button, not inside it.
**Fill rule:** the button carries the action; the line beneath carries what happens next, when, and
at which point the reader is committed. Cramming the terms into the label is what produces a
seven-word button nobody reads.
**Suits** `Conservative B2B (DACH)` — the shape puts the terms outside the label, which is how this
register's demand for a stated commitment point is met without a seven-word button.
**Never** as a social opener or an ad first line — the construction is two parts and only one of
them is the button, so a surface that shows the label alone delivers the ask with its terms
missing.

Two rules govern the whole group, and they are the ones most often broken:

- **A button names the transaction, never the feeling.** A label describing how the reader should
  feel about clicking gets parsed as evasion, and in `Conservative B2B (DACH)` an ambiguous
  commitment point is not asked about — the reader simply does not press it.
- **The platform's default word is not a label.** *Submit* is what the form does, not what the
  reader gets.

**Never** put a question in a button. The reader is not being asked whether to act; if he is, the
page has not finished arguing.

## Filling a slot: three rules

1. **A slot filled with an abstraction has not been filled.** *Efficiency*, *growth*, *peace of
   mind* in a result slot leave the construction doing all the work, and constructions do not
   persuade anyone on their own.
2. **A slot the client has not supplied stays open.** Put the `NOT ESTABLISHED` marker in the slot
   and hand the line over unfinished rather than choosing a plausible figure. The rule and the
   reason for it are in `../SKILL.md`; what this file adds is only that a construction is not a
   licence to fill its own blanks.
3. **Read the filled line without the construction's scaffolding.** Delete *without*, *for*, *no*,
   the colon — if what remains is a claim worth making, the shape was carrying an argument. If
   nothing remains, the shape was the argument, and a different type is needed rather than a
   different template.
