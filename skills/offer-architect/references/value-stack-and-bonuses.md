# The value stack and its bonuses

A stack exists so a reader can do arithmetic and land somewhere near the asking price. That is its
entire job. If the reader cannot add the list up, it is a list of inclusions, not a stack, and it is
doing none of the work it was assembled to do.

This file is about **designing** the stack — what goes in it, what each line is worth, and what to do
about the lines that are not worth anything. How the finished stack gets laid out on a page is
`../../conversion-page-build/references/section-playbooks.md`'s Value stack row, and the two
decisions are separate: what belongs in the stack does not change because the page is a scope table
in one register and a reveal in another.

## The table, and the column most stacks omit

Build the stack as a table with five columns, not as a bulleted list of inclusions:

| Item | Which variable it moves | Stated value, and where that number comes from | Would this buyer pay for this alone? | Deliverable today? |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The fourth column is the one that separates a real stack from a menu, and it is the one almost every
stack skips. Run it honestly and a typical long list resolves into a few items the buyer
would genuinely pay for on their own, and the rest are the cost of being considered at all.

**A "no" in that column is not a reason to remove the item.** Catering, materials, a portal login and
a welcome call all belong in the description of what the buyer receives. They just cannot carry
price. Knowing which lines carry price and which lines only complete the picture is what stops a
stack from being padded with things the reader silently discounts to zero — and the reader does
discount them, immediately, without being told to.

## Every stated value needs a provenance

A number in the third column has to come from somewhere a stranger could check:

- a **real standalone price** — the item is sold separately, today, at that figure;
- a **published comparable** — a competitor sells substantially this thing at that figure, and you
  can point at where;
- a **documented avoided cost** — the buyer would otherwise pay that to someone, and you can say to
  whom for what.

If a line has none of the three, it carries no number. Not a conservative estimate, not a range —
nothing. An invented figure is worse than a blank, because a blank is honest about what the item is
and an invented figure invites the reader to test it against the one number on the page they *can*
check, which is your price.

The same discipline caps the total without needing a ratio rule. Where every figure traces to a real
price, the stack total lands where it lands and can be defended line by line. Where figures are
generated to reach an impressive total, the asymmetry between that total and the asking price becomes
the most noticeable thing on the page, and it reads as fabrication fastest in exactly the registers
that were hardest to win.

## Reading the arithmetic back

Once the table is filled, add up only what a reader could add up — the lines with a real number
against them — and compare that to the price. This is the finding the client actually needs, and it
is usually uncomfortable.

Where one item carries a genuine standalone price and the rest carry nothing, the reader's arithmetic
stops at that one number, and the remainder of the price is resting on whatever the core delivery is
worth against its market comparables. If the core sells elsewhere for a fraction of the asking price
and nothing else on the list carries a defensible figure, that gap is what the page has to answer,
and lunch, materials and a login do not answer it.

Two moves close a gap like that, and one of them is nearly always cheaper than it looks:

- **Finish the unfinished inclusion.** The item that would answer the arithmetic is usually already
  on the list and merely unspecified — approved but undefined, promised but unscoped, intended but
  unowned. Closing one of those is worth more to the stack than adding anything new to it, because it
  converts a line the reader currently discounts to zero into a line that carries price. Specify what
  it is, who builds it and by when, then value it.
- **Reframe a line that is being described as the wrong thing.** An item positioned as hospitality
  that is actually access to a room full of peers the buyer wants to meet is being sold at the wrong
  value entirely. Naming it as what it is costs nothing and moves it from column-four "no" to
  column-four "possibly."

## When items leave a stack

Whatever justified the old price leaves with them. A removed overnight stay, a dropped evening
session, a discontinued bonus — each was carrying some part of the argument for the number, and the
argument does not repair itself. Note explicitly what the removed items were doing, and say what is
now doing that work. Frequently the honest answer is *nothing yet*, and that is a finding.

## Bonuses

A bonus is a stack line whose job is to close one specific hesitation. That framing is Corey Haines's
and Alex Hormozi's both, and it is right: bonuses that exist to bulk out a list are read as bulk.

Three rules make it operational here:

1. **Take the objections from the brief, ranked, and build against the top ones.** The brief's
   Objections section already did the research; a bonus invented against a guessed objection closes
   nothing. Where there is no brief, this is one of the places its absence hurts most — say so.
2. **A bonus that closes an objection the page never raises is invisible.** The reader has to be
   holding the doubt at the moment the bonus appears, or the bonus reads as another item. This is why
   bonus design and objection sequencing are one decision, not two —
   `../../conversion-page-build/references/objection-to-belief.md` is where the sequencing lives.
3. **Prefer bonuses that are cheap to deliver and expensive to fake.** An intake questionnaire, a
   pre-read, a named checkpoint, an artefact the buyer keeps — these cost the business almost nothing
   per buyer and cannot be claimed by a competitor who has not built them. Generic community access
   and a template pack cost nothing to claim, which is why everyone claims them.

Keep the count low. Where a competitor set all offer roughly the same extras, those extras have
stopped being bonuses and become entry requirements, and the stack needs at least one line no
competitor in the benchmark set can answer. Check the benchmark before deciding what is a
differentiator; the answer changes by market and it changes over time.

## What counts as valuable, by register

Register does not change the arithmetic. It changes which kinds of inclusion a reader treats as worth
paying for, which is the fourth column's answer — so read the row for the register the brief named
before filling that column in.

| Register | What this reader will pay for separately | What they discount to zero |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative B2B (DACH) | Anything that reduces the buyer's personal exposure for having recommended you: written scope, a named accountable person, a documented method, a checkpoint with a date on it. | Hospitality, atmosphere, community framing, and any inclusion whose value is asserted rather than priced. Unpriceable extras make the priced ones look assembled. |
| Technical / industrial | Data, tooling, calibration, documentation, and anything that leaves the buyer's own team more capable afterwards. Specifications are inclusions here. | Motivational or experiential components, and support framed as reassurance rather than as a defined response commitment. |
| Consumer / lifestyle | Time saved, a shortcut past a step they dread, and anything they can picture themselves using in the first week. | Anything requiring setup before it pays off, and inclusions described in the seller's vocabulary rather than the buyer's. |
| Mission / nonprofit | Capacity that outlasts the engagement — training, materials the organisation keeps, something a volunteer can run next year without you. | Prestige items, and anything that reads as spend on the organisation rather than on the work. The stack is scrutinised as budget, not as value. |
| Direct-response | Speed to the first result, and anything that removes work between purchase and that result. Standalone-saleable assets carry the most weight because their prices are checkable. | Long-horizon inclusions, and anything whose payoff arrives after the point they would have asked for a refund. |
