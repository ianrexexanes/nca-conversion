# Page architecture

Eleven sections, in one order. This file is the order and the argument for why it's that order —
not another one, and not a random one that happens to be common. What each section has to
accomplish inside a given register lives in `section-playbooks.md`; this file stays one level up,
at the question of sequence and dependency. If you find yourself explaining *how* to write a
section here, that content belongs in the playbook instead — this file only explains *why it comes
where it comes*. The deck sections a full landing page is built from, and the playbooks that write
each, are in `## The long-form order` below.

## The spine underneath the eleven

A reader acts on a page for the same reason they act on anything: they recognize a discomfort, can
picture a better state, and believe a specific next step closes the distance between the two. All
three have to be standing at once, because each is doing a job the others can't cover. Skip the
discomfort and there's nothing established for the reader to be escaping from, so the rest reads as
a pitch aimed at no particular person. Skip the vision and the escape has no destination worth the
trouble of reading further. Establish both and still skip the path, and the reader is left agreeing
with the diagnosis while doubting this specific page is the cure — which is a worse outcome than
never having agreed with the diagnosis at all, because now they're leaving convinced and unsold.

The eleven sections are that three-part shape, run twice: once compressed into a single promise at
the top of the page, then once in full, each part of the promise re-argued with the evidence the
compressed version didn't have room for. Hero states the whole transformation as a claim. Problem
through FAQ then earns it, section by section, in the order a skeptical reader actually needs it
earned — discomfort named, vision made concrete, a path introduced, the path defended, its contents
priced, the defense corroborated, the remaining doubts named and answered, the residual risk
reversed, the ask made, and the leftovers mopped up.

That's also why reordering isn't a style choice. Each section spends evidence the sections before it
built, and promises evidence the sections after it haven't paid out yet. Move one and something
downstream either has nothing to stand on or gets asked to do a job twice.

## The long-form order

A full landing page is built as a deck of sections, in the order below. Each deck section is written
from the playbooks named in its row, in `section-playbooks.md`. The argument itself is the one this
file already made, Hero to FAQ: the deck sections are how that argument is laid out on the page. The
closer adds no new argument. After the FAQ it repeats the promise, the proof and the ask, as a mini
hero. The table gives the cold visitor's order; warm visitors take proof before pain (see Skeleton by
awareness below). The value section holds four to eight value blocks.

| Order | Deck section | What the section does | Playbooks that write it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | header | Orients the visitor and offers the one action; on a landing page it holds no exit | `Header` |
| 2 | hero | States the promise, holds the two proof slots and the button | `Hero` |
| 3 | pain | Names the problem and what it costs, as Problem, Agitate, Solve or the old way against the new | `Problem` |
| 4 | proof | Stacked proof that backs the promise, with the button repeated beside it | `Proof` |
| 5 | value | Four to eight value blocks: the outcome, what the offer is and what is inside it | `Outcome`, `Solution` and `Value stack` |
| 6 | compare | Sets the offer against competitors and the status quo, every claim checkable | `Compare` |
| 7 | how | Shows how it works in three or four steps and what happens next | `Mechanism` |
| 8 | team | Puts the founder or the team on the page, with real names and photos | `Team` |
| 9 | guarantee | Reverses the remaining risk with terms the business can keep | `Guarantee` |
| 10 | faq | Answers what the page has not, built from the buyer's real objections | `FAQ`, its questions from `Objections` |
| 11 | closer | A mini hero: the promise, the proof and the ask again | `Closer` and `CTA` |

## Skeleton by awareness

Awareness picks the skeleton. It is asked beside mode and traffic and is a separate answer: a Direct
page can have warm visitors, so the skeleton follows awareness, not mode.

- **Cold, problem-aware visitors** (outreach, social ads) get an image or video hero. For them pain comes before
  proof: the pain section earns their attention first, and proof follows it.
- **Warm, solution-aware visitors** (search, retargeting, email to known leads) get a form hero. For them proof
  comes before pain: they already know the problem, so they need a reason to trust this answer first.

Both run the long-form order; the hero and the swap of pain and proof are what differ. The hero build in
`../../hook-lab/references/hero-build.md` says the same under By awareness, and `page-types.md` describes
the four skeletons in words.

## Register sensitivity, at a glance

Each section below closes with how much its *execution* — not its presence — moves across the five
registers in `../../audience-research/references/register-profiles.md`. Two sections change almost
nothing about what gets said, only how; a few change what artifact belongs in the section at all.
Treat this rating as a warning about where a register mismatch does real damage to the argument
versus where it only costs a clean edit — the distinction `register-profiles.md` itself draws
between an editing-pass failure and a wrong-argument failure.

### Hero

**Job.** Compress the whole argument — discomfort, vision, path — into what a reader takes in before
they've decided to keep reading. Nothing here is proven yet; it's the claim the rest of the page
exists to earn back.

**Why it's first.** Everything after this section is allowed to assume the reader is still there
because the compressed promise held their attention long enough to keep going. Nothing before it —
there's nothing to build on yet, only traffic and whatever expectation the ad, the search query, or
the referring page already set.

**What it hands off.** The specific discomfort and the specific vision named here are what Problem
and Outcome are about to unpack in full — if Hero promises one transformation and Problem describes
a different one, the reader spends the next two sections wondering if they're on the right page.

**Register sensitivity: medium.** The three-beat promise itself barely moves — every register still
needs discomfort, vision and a path named fast. What moves is how loudly the vision gets stated and
how much of the path gets named this early: a register that treats urgency as a fraud signal cannot
lean on the same compressed excitement a register that expects it uses to earn the scroll.

### Problem

**Job.** Expand the discomfort beat. Make the reader recognize their own situation specifically
enough that a generic pain statement — one that would fit any competitor's page unchanged — reads as
a miss, not a match.

**Why it's second, not first.** A reader has to recognize the problem as theirs before a promised
vision means anything; stated in the other order, Outcome is describing a transformation the reader
hasn't yet agreed they need.

**What it hands off.** The specific frustration named here is the *before* half of the contrast
Outcome is about to complete. A Problem section that stays abstract leaves Outcome with nothing
concrete to transform away from.

**Register sensitivity: low.** This is the section that moves least across registers, because a real
frustration described in the audience's own words is close to register-neutral — a Swiss
procurement officer and a solo consumer buyer both recognize their own problem the same way, in
plain language. What shifts is polish and formality, an editing-pass concern, not an argument one.

### Outcome

**Job.** Make the vision beat concrete — measurable, timeframed, or both — rather than a soft claim
like "save time" that can't be checked and therefore can't be believed.

**Why it's third.** It needs Problem's specific discomfort already on the page to contrast against;
stated before Problem, it's a features list dressed as a benefit, with nothing established yet for
the benefit to be a benefit *from*.

**What it hands off.** The promise this section states in outcome terms is what Solution is about to
name a vehicle for, and what Mechanism will later have to defend as achievable rather than merely
described.

**Register sensitivity: low–medium.** The underlying content — a concrete future state — holds
everywhere; what moves is how far the claim can lean into specificity before it needs a hedge. A
register that audits every checkable claim wants the outcome framed with a stated condition attached
("in a market like this, within X weeks"); a register reading the page as native wants the same
outcome stated flatly, because the hedge itself would read as doubt.

### Solution

**Job.** Name the path. What this actually is, in enough detail that a reader could say what they'd
be buying, without yet defending *why* the path works — that's Mechanism's job, next.

**Why it's fourth.** Naming the vehicle only makes sense once there's a discomfort and a vision on
the page for it to be the bridge between; introduced earlier, it reads as a product announcement
with no argument underneath it yet.

**What it hands off.** Solution names the *what*; everything from here to the CTA is building the
case for *why this one, and why it'll work for someone in the reader's specific situation* — the
doubt Mechanism exists to answer.

**Register sensitivity: low–medium.** What the offer *is* doesn't change by register. How plainly
the category gets named does: a register where jargon is a competence test wants the category named
in the field's own term of art; a register meeting a reader mid-consideration can afford a more
inviting, less categorical name for the same thing.

### Mechanism

**Job.** Answer the doubt Solution deliberately left open — not that this works in general, but that
it works for someone in the reader's specific situation. This is the Value Equation's Perceived
Likelihood of Achievement lever, addressed head-on: naming *how*, not just asserting *that*.

**Why it's fifth.** It has to follow Solution, because there's nothing to explain the workings of
until the thing itself has been named — and it has to precede Value stack and Proof, because neither
an itemized price nor outside evidence means anything if the reader doesn't yet believe the
underlying approach is sound.

**What it hands off.** A credible mechanism is what makes the price in Value stack legible as value
rather than as a number, and what gives Proof something specific to corroborate rather than a vague
claim to vouch for.

**Register sensitivity: medium.** Every register needs a real mechanism — asserting the outcome
without explaining the *how* is a failure everywhere. What moves is the unit of explanation: a
diagram, tolerance or named methodology for a register that treats jargon as a trust signal; a
plainly-stated cause-and-effect sentence for a register that would read a diagram as a distraction
from the feeling being sold.

### Value stack

**Job.** Show what's actually inside the path, itemized, so the reader is comparing the price to
something concrete rather than to an undifferentiated bundle. This is where anchoring and contrast —
what this replaces, what it would cost pieced together, what inaction costs — do their work.

**Why it's sixth.** Itemizing only makes sense once Mechanism has made the reader believe the
approach works; stack the components before that belief exists and the reader is pricing something
they're not yet sure does anything.

**What it hands off.** The concrete components named here are exactly what Proof is about to
corroborate one at a time — a value stack with nothing specific in it leaves Proof with nothing to
attach evidence to.

**Register sensitivity: high.** This is one of the three sections — with Guarantee and CTA — where a
register mismatch changes the argument, not just the prose. A stacked, escalating reveal that reads
as momentum in one register reads as a hard sell that a serious buyer cannot forward to a colleague
in another; a plain scope table that reads as trustworthy restraint in one register reads as
under-selling in another. `section-playbooks.md` treats this section's five registers as five
different constructions, not five tones on one construction.

### Proof

**Job.** Corroborate the mechanism and the stack with evidence the reader didn't have to take on
faith — raising Perceived Likelihood of Achievement a second time, now with something outside the
seller's own claims.

**Why it's seventh.** Proof needs the Mechanism and Value stack already on the page to corroborate;
placed earlier, it's evidence for a claim the reader hasn't been shown yet, which reads as generic
trust signaling rather than as support for anything specific.

**What it hands off.** The credibility this section earns is the reserve Objections is about to
spend — an objection answer that says "and here's a case where that concern didn't hold" only works
if the reader already believes the case is real, which is exactly what this section establishes.

**Register sensitivity: high.** `register-profiles.md` gives Proof its own fully worked-out
dimension per register precisely because *what counts as evidence* is not a matter of degree here —
a named person with a checkable role, a datasheet with a test-standard citation, an unsourced
photograph, an audited account, and a screenshot with a visible date range are five different
artifact types, not five deliveries of the same artifact. Getting the artifact type wrong here is a
wrong-argument failure, not a prose one.

### Objections

**Job.** Take the brief's ranked Objections section and, for each one, walk surface objection to
hidden objection to belief shift to proof — see `objection-to-belief.md` for the chain in full. Name
the load-bearing objections a buyer in this category actually carries, not a generic reassurance
list.

**Why it's eighth.** It needs Proof already on the page, because a belief shift with nothing to
attach it to is just an assertion that the reader's doubt is wrong — the same failure a missing
Mechanism causes, one section later. It has to precede Guarantee and CTA because asking for the sale
before the reasons not to buy have been named leaves exactly those reasons live in the reader's head
at the moment they're deciding.

**What it hands off.** Whatever objection this section can't fully resolve on the page is what
Guarantee is about to reverse structurally instead of arguing away, and what FAQ mops up at lower
priority.

**Register sensitivity: medium.** Which objections get named is mostly audience-driven, not
register-driven — the brief's ranked list travels unchanged. What register decides is how directly
the shift gets narrated: some registers can say "you might be thinking X" out loud and answer it in
the same breath; others need the proof to imply the same shift without ever naming the reader's
doubt back to them, because the narration itself would read as a sales technique being run on them.

### Guarantee

**Job.** Put some of the remaining risk back on the seller — the Effort & Sacrifice lever's emotional
half — rather than leaving the entire downside of being wrong with the buyer.

**Why it's ninth, after Objections and not before.** A guarantee introduced before the case has been
made reads as compensation for a weak argument; introduced after Proof and Objections have already
done the persuading, it reads as confidence backed by everything the reader has already seen. Strong
proof plus a guarantee reinforces; a guarantee standing in for proof that was never built undermines
itself the moment a reader notices the substitution.

**What it hands off.** Whatever confidence this section adds is what the CTA, next, is allowed to
spend on a direct ask — a page that asks before reversing the risk is asking the reader to carry
resistance the guarantee exists to remove.

**Register sensitivity: high.** Guarantee *type*, not just guarantee wording, changes by register:
an unconditional refund, a conditional one gated on completed effort, a named SLA, and a deliberate
anti-guarantee that reads as confidence to a mature buyer and as cold to a first-time one are
different instruments for different buying situations, not the same instrument phrased four ways.
Picking the wrong type here is the section's wrong-argument failure; wording it well in the right
type is the editing pass.

### CTA

**Job.** Ask. One action, worded as the outcome of clicking rather than the mechanics of the click,
placed only after every load-bearing resistance above it has been named and answered.

**Why it's tenth.** Every section before this one exists to make this ask safe to make. A CTA
appearing earlier in the page isn't wrong to include — repeating the primary action at natural
stopping points is expected — but the *primary*, argument-closing ask belongs here, once the
resistance the earlier sections were built to remove has actually been removed.

**What it hands off.** Whatever the button asks for is the commitment FAQ, last, has to protect —
a well-placed CTA that gets undermined by an unresolved question two sections later has still lost
the reader.

**Register sensitivity: high.** Directness itself is register-defined, not a copywriting choice
layered on top of a fixed action: one commanded action with explicit risk reversal for a reader
deciding alone in one session. Two registers can want more than the one action. A reader who has to
bring this to someone else may be given a lower-commitment second option beside the primary one, and an
audience that is legitimately split may be given several parallel, non-competing participation paths.
Both are a register's departure from the one action, written in the deck's `## Register notes` (the
rule, what changed, why), and the user decides per page. Using the wrong shape is a structural
mismatch, not a wording problem.

### FAQ

**Job.** Answer the objections that didn't carry enough weight to interrupt the main argument, plus
whatever a search engine or an AI answer surface would reasonably expect a page like this to cover.

**Why it's last.** Everything load-bearing has already been resolved by Objections and Guarantee;
what's left is real but lower-priority, which is exactly the material a reader only goes looking for
after they've already decided to take the page seriously.

**What it hands off.** Nothing — this is the end of the page's argument. Its job is to not leave a
real, findable question unanswered after everything above it has done its work.

**Register sensitivity: medium.** The question set stays close to what Objections didn't already
resolve, which is audience-driven more than register-driven. What moves is formality and directness
of the answers, in step with the rest of the page — plus, orthogonally to register entirely, whether
the questions are marked up as `FAQPage` schema, which is a technical-SEO concern this file doesn't
own.
