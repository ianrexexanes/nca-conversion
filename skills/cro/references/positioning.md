# Positioning

Read this when `/cro research` finds a positioning gap in its seventh step, and when the page leg of `/cro diagnose` scores "What's in it for me?" or "Is it for me?" low. Claude reads it and puts each question to the owner, or answers it from the research; it never makes up an answer. Positioning is the choice of what a visitor compares the offer with and why it wins, and it comes before any wording, so a page whose headline changes every week usually has none underneath it. Work through the sections in order, because each rests on the one before.

## Competitive alternatives

The question: what would the best customers do if this offer did not exist? Ask the customers, not a list of rival firms. The honest answers are often "nothing", a spreadsheet, a relative who helps out, a general tool, a hire in house, or a firm down the road. Rank them by how often customers actually pick them, drop the ones nobody mentions, and group the rest.

A gap here looks like a page that compares the offer with the wrong thing, or with nothing at all. The visitor cannot tell what they are choosing between, so every objection comes as a surprise. Example: a plumbing firm's customers would otherwise ring a friend who does odd jobs, wait for the landlord, or call the first firm on the map. The page has to beat those habits, not "other plumbers".

## What is unique

The question: what does the offer have or do that those alternatives do not? It can be a feature, a way of working, a skill, a guarantee, a local presence, a partner. Each one needs proof a visitor could check, such as a customer's own words, a record, or a third party. Keep the attributes that decide whether someone buys, and leave out what matters only after the purchase.

A gap here looks like an adjective with nothing behind it ("the best in town") or a claim every rival makes too. Example: a bookkeeping practice that finishes the monthly books by a day it states in writing and shows its working has something a visitor can test. A practice that only says it is "honest and dependable" has nothing.

## The value it enables

The question: so what? Take each unique attribute and ask what it lets the customer do, then what that is worth to them, in their words. Stop when you reach something the customer would say themselves. Group the results into a few themes, sometimes only one.

A gap here looks like a headline that says what the offer is instead of what the visitor can do afterwards. Example: a dental clinic that scans instead of taking moulds. The attribute is the scanner, the benefit is a comfortable visit, and the value is one appointment with no temporary crown to look after.

## Who cares most

The question: which customers understand it at once, buy quickly, accept the price without pushing for a discount, and tell others? Describe them by what they do or have in common that makes them care, such as a situation, a habit or a tool they already use, and not by age or job title alone. A segment is worth using when it is big enough for the owner's goals and shares an important need nobody meets well. Narrow beats broad, and the owner widens only when the narrow group cannot reach the goal. When nobody has bought yet, these are guesses, and the brief says so.

A gap here looks like a page that speaks to "businesses" or "homeowners" in general, so no reader feels singled out. Example: a bookkeeping practice for sole-trader plumbers who have just taken on their first employee has a reader who knows at a glance the page is for them.

## The market category

The question: what does the visitor call this kind of offer, and what frame makes its value obvious? The category decides which rivals they compare it with and what they count as good and as expensive. The owner has these choices:

- Compete inside a category that already exists, by being best at the one thing buyers weigh most.
- Narrow to a slice of an existing category where the strengths count most. For a small firm this is often the practical choice.
- Name a new category. It is rare and costly, because the market has to be taught what it means.

Check the frame by listening. When it works, visitors say back what the offer is without being told, the right rivals come up, and sales calls skip "what exactly are you?".

A gap here looks like an offer called by the owner's own label, so visitors compare it with the wrong thing or with nothing. Example: a clinic that calls itself a "wellness hub" gets compared with spas, when its best customers want a dentist for nervous patients.

## How each feeds the page

Each part has a place on the page. The part that is missing is the part to fix first.

- The value it enables feeds the `H1` and the line under it, which answer "What's in it for me?".
- Who cares most feeds "Is it for me?": the first screen names the reader's situation in the words they use.
- The market category is what the page calls the offer, in the title, the headline and the button.
- The competitive alternatives feed the comparison and the objections.
- What is unique feeds the proof.

A part the owner cannot state goes into the brief's `## Open and unconfirmed` list, with what it would take to settle it. It is never filled in from a guess.
