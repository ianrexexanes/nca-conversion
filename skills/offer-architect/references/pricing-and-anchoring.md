# Pricing and anchoring

**This file recommends. It never sets a price.** The number belongs to whoever carries the cost side,
the cash position and the consequence of getting it wrong, and that is the client. What is produced
here is an argument about how a price *reads* and what it can be held to — which is a different
question from what it should be, and a more useful one to hand over.

## Establish the cost side, or say you couldn't

A price can be evaluated two ways. **Structurally** — does the construction hold up, is the anchor
credible, does the stack support the number — needs nothing but the offer. **Commercially** — is this
profitable, where is break-even, how many units make the delivery viable — needs a cost model, and
without one no break-even figure exists no matter how confident the surrounding analysis sounds.

Check whether an existing cost model still applies before relying on it. A model built for one shape
of delivery does not survive a change of shape: splitting one event into two removes some costs and
adds a whole second occurrence of others, and any new component that has to be built is a cost the
old model never carried. Where the model is stale, write
`NOT ESTABLISHED — <what re-costing would take>` and scope every conclusion to the structural read.
Saying which of the two evaluations you actually ran is the honest part; clients assume the second
one unless told.

## Where a price can sit

Four reference points bound the answer, and only two of them are real constraints:

- **The next-best alternative** is the floor for anything undifferentiated. A buyer who sees no
  difference pays the lower number.
- **What the buyer believes the outcome is worth to them** is the ceiling. Not what it is worth — what
  they believe, at the moment of purchase.
- **Cost to serve** is a survival floor and nothing more. It tells you what you cannot go below; it
  says nothing about what you can charge.
- **Competitor prices** are worth knowing and are not a number to aim at. Matching one adopts a
  strategy whose economics you cannot see and may not share.

This ordering is Corey Haines's `pricing` skill's, and it holds. The addition worth making: the
ceiling is a *belief*, so it is moved by the same four variables as everything else in
`value-equation.md`. Most requests to lower a price are requests to raise perceived likelihood or cut
time delay wearing different clothes, and it is worth checking which one you actually have before
touching the number.

## Does the anchor read as credible, or as pressure?

Anchoring means placing the price next to another number so the reader has something to compare it
to. The construction almost always exists already in a real offer — an early-commitment price, a
struck-through figure, a stack total. The useful question is not whether to anchor but which of the
two ways the existing anchor is being read.

**What makes an anchor credible:**

- The second number is real and published, not a fiction with a line through it.
- There is a date, and it is a specific one on a named day rather than a rolling "this week."
- The difference is arithmetic the reader can do himself.
- **There is a stated mechanism for why the early price exists.** This is the one that is almost
  always missing and almost always cheap to add. Early commitments that make a booking possible,
  fund a production run, or fill a minimum are honest reasons, they are checkable in principle, and
  stating one converts the tier from a lever into an explanation.

**What makes it read as pressure — count the signals.** Take the price block and count the distinct
scarcity or urgency devices visible in it at the same time: an early tier, a dated deadline, a
capacity cap, a "this will not run again," a timer, a live counter. One device is a mechanism.
Several stacked together is the signature of a seller who does not trust the offer, and at a serious
price that reading costs more than the urgency earns. Pick the axis with the most external backing —
usually a date, because a date is verifiable and does not depend on the seller's word — and let the
remaining numbers appear as facts about the format rather than as pressure. A capacity number is
better presented as the reason the delivery works than as a countdown.

**A scarcity number that has moved in public is a number nobody believes**, and its movement casts
doubt on the numbers that were always true. Check whether any published figure has changed before
building an argument on it.

## The anchor's expiry problem

This one is rarely raised and it costs real money. **An anchor only functions while the number it is
compared against is still visible.** The day an early tier closes, a buyer arriving on the page sees
one figure and nothing beside it — and that is frequently a large share of the selling window running
against a price with no comparison at all.

So ask, every time an offer has a dated tier: what justifies the number *after* the deadline? Usually
exactly one line in the stack is capable of carrying that job — the one item with a real published
standalone price. Which is an argument for promoting that item into the price block *before* the
deadline passes, so the reader has something to compare against for the rest of the window, rather
than discovering the gap afterwards.

## Tiers

Three is the working default: an entry rung, the one most buyers should land on, and one above it
that makes the middle look reasonable. Two is fine where the segments genuinely split in two. Four or
more buys granularity and pays for it in deliberation, and deliberation is the thing a price block
exists to end.

Differentiate on something the buyer can feel — how much of the work you do, how fast, how much
access — not on a feature list. `delivery-models.md` is where those differences actually come from:
the cleanest tiering is usually one promise delivered at three different levels of involvement, and
that reads as a coherent ladder rather than as three arbitrary bundles.

## Payment structure is a separate lever from price level

Same total, different structure, different conversion — and the structure is frequently the fix when
the number is not the problem. Instalments widen the buyer pool and add collection risk. A deposit
plus a balance at a milestone ties payment to delivery and suits engagements that run long. Payment
after a result suits high-confidence delivery and hurts cash flow.

One thing to confirm rather than infer: **when the money is actually collected.** A stated total for
a multi-part delivery says nothing about whether it is invoiced once up front or split across the
parts, and the difference changes the buyer's commitment, the cancellation exposure and whatever
booking system has to generate the invoice. Where it is unconfirmed, carry it as an explicit
assumption with what changes if it is wrong. The same goes for whether a published figure includes
tax — restate every derived number if that reading flips.

## By register

The four reference points do not move. What moves is which instruments can appear next to the number.

| Register | Anchoring and price presentation | Scarcity tolerance |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative B2B (DACH) | The price is published, in a round figure, with its scope attached. Anchor against a stated mechanism and against what the buyer would otherwise spend internally. Charm endings read as retail and cheapen a serious number. | One axis, dated and external. No timers, no live counters — in this register those read as a scam signal regardless of whether they are accurate. |
| Technical / industrial | The price appears as a line item with what it covers and what it excludes. Anchor against total cost over the asset's life rather than against a struck-through figure. | Low, and only where the constraint is a real production or scheduling fact the buyer would recognise from his own operation. |
| Consumer / lifestyle | Anchor against something the buyer already spends on without thinking. Charm endings are native here and do not cost credibility. | High tolerance, on the condition that the constraint is true and enforced. This register notices a deadline that does not hold faster than any other, because its buyers see more of them. |
| Mission / nonprofit | Frame the number as a unit of what it accomplishes rather than as a discount from something larger. Comparison anchors read as commercial pressure applied to a non-commercial decision. | Only where a genuine deadline exists — a season, a matching window, an event. A manufactured one is read as manipulation of goodwill and is not recoverable. |
| Direct-response | Full instrumentation is the convention: stack total stated before the price, contrast made explicit, payment options shown. | Highest, and the constraint here is truthfulness rather than restraint. A device that does not hold — a timer that resets, a count that never moves — converts once and is remembered. |

Whatever the register, the underlying limit has to be true and you have to be prepared to hold it. A
deadline that passes without the price moving teaches everyone watching that the next one will not
hold either. Where no genuine limit exists, ship none — an invented one is the most expensive
shortcut available in this file.
