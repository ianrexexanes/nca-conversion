# Lead sources

Read this for the traffic leg of `/cro diagnose`: where visitors come from, what they already know when they land, and whether the page keeps the promise made before the click. Claude reads it, asks the owner the questions in the last section, and reports what the leg could see from outside. The owner's answers are the owner's word and are marked that way; the leg never invents a source.

## The four ways leads arrive

Every lead arrives one of four ways. A pair of questions sorts them: does the sender already know the business, and is the contact one person at a time or one message for many. Each way brings a visitor who knows a different amount, so each needs something different from the page. The awareness and temperature words are the ones `/cro research` uses in its second step.

- **One-to-one contact with people who already know the business.** Past customers, friends, contacts who answer a personal note or a referral. Awareness is warm and temperature is warm or hot. They know who the business is and often what it does. The page confirms quickly and makes the next step easy. It does not explain from the beginning.
- **One-to-one contact with people who do not know the business.** A note, call or message to a stranger. Awareness is cold and temperature is cold. The visitor knows only what that message said. The first screen says who this is and why the note reached them, in the words the note used, and offers a small first step.
- **Free content published for many.** Articles, posts, videos, talks. Awareness runs from cold to warm and temperature from cold to warm. The visitor knows the problem and one piece of the owner's thinking. The page continues the topic they came from, and the next step is in proportion to the trust earned so far.
- **Paid ads.** Awareness is cold and temperature is cold. The visitor knows only what the ad said, read in a few seconds. The headline repeats the ad's promise in the ad's words, and there is one next step. A mismatch costs money, because the owner paid for every visitor who leaves.

A business usually starts with the first way and adds one more. When it runs all of them at once, nobody can say which one works. If the owner cannot name the best source, that is a finding.

## Lead magnets

A lead magnet is a free, small first step that solves a narrow part of the visitor's problem and leads to the paid step. It suits cold traffic, which is why `/cro research` pairs a small first step with cold temperature. For warm traffic the step is deeper, and for hot traffic it is the paid step itself; a free extra in front of someone ready to buy only adds a step.

Kinds, in plain words:

- A diagnosis that shows the visitor a problem they did not know they had, such as a self-check or a test of their current setup.
- A sample or trial of the real service, such as a first lesson, a trial period or a demonstration.
- One step out of a longer process, such as a template, a calculator or a checklist.

Whatever the kind, the traffic leg looks for the same things. The name says who it is for and what they get. It is quick to use, and good enough that the visitor would value it. It ends with a clear route to the paid step. Example: a plumbing firm's printable leak self-check for a home, which ends with a button to book a visit. A gap looks like a generic "free guide" button that names neither the reader nor the result.

## How the traffic leg reads them

For each way, the leg asks the owner, looks at what is public, and checks whether the page keeps the promise made before the click. What it cannot see from outside it lists under could not check.

### Contact with people who know the business

- Ask: how do these people reach the page, by a link, a phone call or a search for the name? What do you tell them before they arrive?
- Seen from outside: whether a contact route sits near the top, and whether the page makes a returning visitor read an introduction before the next step.
- Promise kept when the page lets a person who was told "go and book" do exactly that.

### Contact with strangers

- Ask: for a sample of the note and the link in it. The leg cannot see sent messages, so the sample is the owner's word.
- Seen from outside: whether the first screen says who the business is and for whom, in words that fit a note from a stranger.
- Promise kept when the page opens with the reason the note gave for writing.

### Free content

- Ask: which pieces bring enquiries, and where each one sends the reader.
- Seen from outside: the published piece itself, how it ends, and whether its link lands on a page about the same topic.
- Promise kept when the page picks up where the piece stopped, rather than starting a new subject.

### Paid ads

- Ask: which ads run, where they send people, and what a lead costs against what a customer is worth to the owner.
- Seen from outside: the ad's wording and picture where the platform publishes a public library of ads, and the page the ad lands on.
- Promise kept when the headline repeats the ad's promise and the action matches the ad's call to action.

A source the leg cannot name goes into the report as a question for the owner, not a guess.
