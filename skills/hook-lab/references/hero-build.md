# The hero build

How to write the hero of a page: the headline, the subheadline, the two proof slots and the button. This is
the method for a whole hero. The line-level craft stays in `headline-formulas.md`, and the eight hook types in
`hook-taxonomy.md` choose the angle inside each part below. The section's job on the page is still set in
`../../conversion-page-build/references/section-playbooks.md`.

Read the Conversion Brief first. Every figure, proof item and objection below comes from it. A slot the brief
does not fill stays open (see the proof slots) and is reported, never filled.

## The headline build

Three parts, in this order: the end result, made concrete with a figure the brief supports; how long it
takes; and how the buyer feels once it is done. The type chosen from the eight gives the angle, and the
three parts give the shape. If the brief holds no figure for the result, put the `NOT ESTABLISHED` marker
from `../SKILL.md` in the slot and say what would close it.

Where one fear stops the click, build a second headline that ends on that fear instead of the feeling: the
end result, how long it takes, and "without" the objection the brief records. Use the objection in the
buyer's own words, and only one. Offer the two builds side by side when the brief names such a fear, so the
client picks the angle.

A Search page keeps its H1 rule (see By mode): the keyword near the front, with the promise. The build
supplies the promise.

Example: a bike workshop. Build one: "Shifting that feels new again in two days, and a bike you trust on
the morning ride." Build two: "Shifting that feels new again in two days, without a surprise bill."

## The subheadline build

Move from pain to solution. Open with what the visitor is tired of, or the old way they want to stop, in
the words the brief records. Then say how the offer delivers the headline's promise, naming two or three
ways it does.

The subheadline explains the headline. It does not repeat it and does not add a second promise.

Example: a bike workshop. "Tired of waiting weeks for a slot? We book you in online, fix it at a price
agreed up front, and message you at each step."

## The two proof slots

The hero holds two proof slots, both filled from the brief.

- **`prooftop`.** The first slot. On a Direct page it is a line above the H1: an audience call-out, or the
  strongest proof the brief holds. Set it in the body face, sentence case and untracked, never a pill and
  never tracked capitals. A short line above a heading departs from the common design rule against a small
  label over a heading, so on a Direct page it is a recorded departure: the design step writes it
  down as a ruling line with its reason. On a Search page `prooftop` sits directly under the H1 block (the H1 and
  its answer-first sentence), so that rule holds and no departure is recorded.
- **The proof strip.** The second slot, under the button. It carries a second kind of proof, not a repeat of
  `prooftop`: if the first is a rating, the strip can be named clients or a badge, and the other way round.

Both come from the brief. An empty slot holds `[NEEDED: <what the brief must supply>]` and the output
reports it. The rule is to never invent proof to fill a slot: no rating, count, name, logo or quote that the
brief does not hold.

Example: a dental practice, Direct page. `prooftop`: "For parents booking a first dental visit."

## The button

The label names what the reader gets or the concrete next step. It never uses the seller's own name for the
offer: a label that leaves the reader asking "so what?" fails the so what test, because they cannot tell what
happens when they click. Name the artefact or the result.

- **Length.** The target is two to five words. A label of six or seven is a warning, kept only when the
  outcome needs the words. One word, or eight or more, fails.
- **Voice.** Use the first person ("my") where it fits the register: it puts the reader inside the result.
  Where the offer has something free, say what is free in the label or the line under it.
- **Never generic.** Submit, Contact us, Learn more, Get started and their equivalents in the page's
  language are not labels. They describe the form or the website, not what the reader gets.
- **Variants.** When the hero is tested in variants, keep one label per variant, and let it complete its own
  headline. Inside a variant every button on the page carries the same words, and the
  doubt reducers name the commitment point (`cta.fud` in the page deck), so every variant describes the same
  click.

Put doubt reducers under the button. They are there because they answer the worry that stops the click:
what it costs, what it commits the reader to, and what happens next. The reader decides at the moment of the
click, so the answer has to sit at that moment, in plain words and with no claim the brief cannot back.

Example: an accounting firm offering a free first review. Label: "Book my free review". Under it: "A short
call, nothing to pay, no obligation to hire us."

## By mode

The mode is decided per page (Search or Direct) and changes how much of the build the hero carries.

- **Search page.** Put the keyword near the front of the one H1, together with the promise, and make it one
  claim. Aim for two lines on desktop; three or four lines on a phone is fine. The subheadline is one or two
  sentences, with no stacked benefits. The keyword and the promise are never cut to meet a design limit.
- **Direct page.** Use the full build. The hero can carry stacked benefit lines, up to five, and the headline
  continues the ad or message that earned the click: the ad's promise, the outreach or email line, or the
  phrase searched for a paid search ad. No keyword placement.

## By awareness

Awareness is asked beside mode and traffic, and it is a separate answer: a Direct page can have warm
visitors. The brief records it with the traffic's temperature (cold, warm or hot).

- **Cold visitors**, problem-aware and arriving from outreach or social ads, get an image or video hero.
  The pain section follows it (Problem, Agitate, Solve, or the old way set against the new one).
- **Warm visitors**, solution-aware and arriving from search, retargeting or email to known leads, get a
  form hero.

The offer in the button follows the temperature: a small first step for cold visitors, a deeper one for warm
visitors, and the paid step itself for hot ones.

## Register translation

The build comes from direct response. A register can change what a beat becomes, so translate it before
writing, and state each change (see Register notes). Read the register's own urgency and button sections in
`../../audience-research/references/register-profiles.md` for the full rule.

| Register | Time and urgency | How the buyer feels | Stacked benefits | Button voice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conservative B2B (DACH) | A real capacity or date, never a countdown | Relief stated as a fact about the work, not excitement | Only as plain factual lines, each with its proof | Names what happens next, formal, the same words every time |
| Technical / industrial | Only dated facts such as a lead time or a notice | Confidence that the specification can be checked | Yes, as specification lines with the operating condition | Names the document or the technical conversation |
| Consumer / lifestyle | Real seasons, booking windows or remaining stock, said once | What it will be like, in sensory words | Fits, kept short and sensory | Plain action with the price visible |
| Mission / nonprofit | A real deadline with its reason, nothing more | Dignity first, the change made, never the condition as a lever | Only per audience, never one list for all | Says what the gift or the time does, one path per audience |
| Direct-response | Explicit: a real deadline, cap or dated price step, kept | The full emotional payoff, said outright | Fits, up to five lines | Commanded, first person, terms and guarantee beside it |

## Register notes

When a register changes one of the rules above, the output says so in one line: the rule, what changed and
why. The user then decides per page. When the register changed nothing, one line says that too, so silence
never hides an override.

```
- <rule>: <what changed>, because <why>.
- None: the register changed no rule.
```

Example: "- Urgency: a countdown became the real monthly capacity, because this register reads a timer as a
device."
