# Voice-of-customer mining

The Voice of customer section exists to capture the ICP's own words for their own problem, not the
vendor's internal vocabulary translated into what sounds like customer language. This file covers
where that language actually lives and the discipline required to keep it verbatim once found.

## Where it actually lives

Ranked by how unfiltered the language is — the closer a source is to a customer talking to another
customer or to nobody, the less it has been shaped by an audience they're trying to impress.

**Support tickets and chat transcripts.** The customer is frustrated, specific and not performing
for anyone. This is usually the single richest source and the most under-used, because it sits in a
helpdesk tool nobody thinks to search for marketing material.

**Sales call recordings and notes.** A prospect stating their problem out loud, unprompted, before
the sales rep has reframed it into the company's terminology. The moment right before a rep says
"so what I'm hearing is" is the moment to quote — after that line, the words are the rep's.

**Reviews with real detail** (G2, Trustpilot, Capterra, App Store, Amazon, Google). Written for
other buyers, which means the vocabulary is calibrated to persuade someone in the same position —
exactly the calibration a landing page needs. Skim past the one-line five-stars; the review with
three specific sentences is worth more than ten with none.

**Forums, communities and comment threads** where the ICP talks to itself with no vendor present at
all — a subreddit, an industry Slack, comments under a competitor's LinkedIn post. This is the
lowest-performance source per hour spent (a lot of noise) and the highest-trust source per finding,
because there is no vendor in the room to perform for.

**Churn and cancellation reasons**, wherever the client captures them. Usually thin on volume and
disproportionately valuable, because it's the only source that reliably surfaces the objection that
actually loses deals rather than the one prospects say out loud in a sales call to be polite.

**What to deprioritize:** the client's own marketing copy, internal positioning docs, and answers to
"how would you describe your customer" asked directly of the client. These are hypotheses about the
customer's voice, not the voice itself — useful as a starting point for Mode 3 (see
`research-modes.md`), never as a substitute for the sources above.

## Keeping it verbatim

The entire value of this section collapses the moment a real phrase gets smoothed into something
that reads better. A smoothed phrase is indistinguishable from something the researcher invented,
and once one line in the section is suspect, none of it can be trusted downstream — page copy built
on it inherits words nobody actually said.

**Quote, don't summarize.** "We keep guessing and getting it wrong" goes in the brief exactly as
written. "Customer reported inconsistent forecasting accuracy" is not a paraphrase of that quote,
it's a different, weaker fact — the vendor's category label wearing the customer's sentence as a
disguise.

**Fix nothing.** Typos, grammar, capitalization, punctuation stay as the customer wrote or said
them. A `[sic]` is unnecessary in a working document; this brief is not being published, it's being
mined. If a quote is long, trim it with an ellipsis rather than tidy it — cutting is honest,
smoothing is not.

**Attribute the source, not the identity.** Log where each quote came from — "G2 review, competitor
product, 2024" or "sales call notes, deal lost Q2" — without needing the person's name in most
cases. The source matters for judging how much weight the quote carries; the identity usually
doesn't, and skipping it removes a reason to hesitate over privacy on material that's often already
public or already anonymized by the tool that captured it.

**Separate the quote from the pattern.** When several quotes converge on the same frame — the ICP
consistently describes the problem as something that happens *to* them rather than something they
manage — name that pattern in a sentence of your own, next to the quotes that support it, rather
than folding your interpretation into the quote itself. The brief should let a reader tell instantly
which words are the customer's and which are the researcher's read on them.

## The failure this discipline prevents

A brief with a Voice of customer section full of clean, complete sentences is not evidence that
research went well — it's usually evidence that a researcher paraphrased on the way in. Real
customer language is often a fragment, often ends mid-thought, and often uses a word the client's
own product page would never use. If every quote in the section reads like ad copy already, treat
that as a signal to go back to source rather than as a sign the mining is done.
