# Research modes

Three modes, cheapest first. Each is exhausted at a specific, checkable point — read that point
before starting, so you know in advance what escalating looks like instead of discovering it by
running out of ideas.

## Mode 1 — Existing material

**What it is.** Everything the client already has, sitting unread: past campaign briefs, sales call
notes or recordings, CRM notes, support tickets, onboarding call transcripts, existing landing
pages and their analytics, a previous agency's research, internal Slack threads where someone
already argued about who the buyer is. This is not new research — it is reading research that
already happened and was never assembled into a brief.

**What it yields.** The Problems (ranked) and Objections sections almost fully, because sales and
support conversations are where a prospect states their problem and their hesitation in their own
words, unprompted. Often a rough ICP and a first cut at Competitive landscape, because a sales team
usually already knows who they're being compared against even if nobody wrote it down.

**What it costs.** Time to read, not time to gather — the material exists. The real cost is
attention: a CRM export or a folder of call notes is unstructured, and pulling ranked signal out of
it takes longer than it looks like it should. Budget for reading, not for outreach.

**When it's exhausted.** Read everything available and Voice of customer is still thin, or the
material only ever covers won deals — never lost ones, never people who churned, never people who
looked and walked away. A brief built entirely from won-deal material over-indexes on the pitch
that already works and tells you nothing about the objection that killed the deals you never see.
That gap is the signal to escalate.

## Mode 2 — Public and passive sources

**What it is.** Sources the ICP wrote in their own words with no researcher present: reviews (G2,
Trustpilot, Capterra, App Store, Amazon), forum and community threads (Reddit, industry-specific
forums, LinkedIn comments under a competitor's post), competitor pages and their own reviews, search
autocomplete and "people also ask" for the problem category, support-ticket language if the client
publishes a help center.

**What it yields.** Voice of customer at real depth — this is the primary source for that section,
because a review or forum post is written for other buyers, not for a vendor, and carries none of
the flattening that happens when someone answers a researcher's question. Differentiation gets
sharper here too: a competitor's one-star reviews name the exact gap a differentiation claim needs
to close.

**What it costs.** No access required, but real time — reading fifty reviews to find the six that
say something specific is normal, and forum threads run long before they run useful. It does not
require the client's involvement, which makes it the right mode to run before the first client call
so that call can verify rather than discover.

**When it's exhausted.** The same three or four phrases keep recurring across sources and new
sources stop adding vocabulary — that saturation point is a good sign, not a stall, and it means
Voice of customer is probably done. Escalate when what's missing is not language but *decision
process*: who else has to sign off, what happens between "interested" and "bought," what almost
stopped the sale. Public sources rarely carry that; only the people who ran the sale do.

## Mode 3 — Direct client and customer contact

**What it is.** A structured conversation with the client about their own buyers (not a generic
questionnaire — ask what a customer said, verbatim, the last time a deal was won and the last time
one was lost), and where possible a short conversation with an actual current or recent customer.
This is the only mode that can fill Proof available with certainty, because it's the only one where
you can ask "can you actually produce that" and get a real answer.

**What it yields.** Everything the first two modes couldn't reach: the buyer/attendee split when
one exists (see `persona-and-jtbd.md`), what proof the client can actually produce versus what they
wish they could, and confirmation or correction of everything Modes 1 and 2 inferred. Treat this
mode as verification as much as discovery — bring a draft brief into the conversation and ask the
client to correct it, rather than starting from a blank page.

**What it costs.** The client's or a customer's actual time, which is the scarcest resource in this
list and the reason this mode runs last, not first. It also carries a bias risk Modes 1 and 2
don't: a client describing their own buyer tends to describe the buyer they want, not the one who
actually converts, which is exactly why this mode should correct the first two drafts rather than
originate the brief from scratch.

**When it's exhausted.** It generally isn't — this mode ends when the brief's `Open and
unconfirmed` list stops shrinking between conversations, not when a fixed amount of contact has
happened. What's left after that point goes into `Open and unconfirmed` as `NOT ESTABLISHED`
rather than being guessed at to close the section.
