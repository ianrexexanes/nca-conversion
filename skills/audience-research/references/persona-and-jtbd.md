# Personas and jobs-to-be-done

The Personas section names who inside the ICP actually reads the page, in what role. This file
covers how to build a persona around the job it's hired to do rather than a demographic sketch, and
the one structural split that a persona list most often collapses by accident.

## Build around the job, not the profile

A persona written as a demographic sketch — age, title, a few interests — describes a person
without saying what the page needs to do for them. A persona written around jobs-to-be-done starts
from the job the offer is "hired" to perform: the progress this person is trying to make, the
circumstance that made them start looking, and what they'll fire in favor of this offer if it
works.

For each persona, establish:

- **The job.** What progress are they trying to make? Not "wants better software" but "needs to
  stop finding out about a stockout from an angry customer" — specific enough that a competing
  option (including doing nothing) can be judged against it.
- **The trigger.** What changed that put them in-market today rather than last quarter? A job can
  exist unaddressed for years; the trigger is why this page might reach them at the moment it
  matters.
- **What they're firing.** The status quo they'd abandon if this works — a spreadsheet, a
  competitor, an internal workaround, inaction. Naming it sharpens Differentiation and Objections
  more directly than a features comparison does, because it states what the offer is actually
  competing against in this person's head.
- **What "hired and it worked" looks like to them**, specifically — this is what a testimonial or
  proof point needs to demonstrate to land with this persona.

## The buyer/attendee distinction

Many offers are consumed by one person and paid for by another, and those two people are doing
different jobs, evaluating different evidence, and objecting for different reasons. This shows up
under different names depending on the category — buyer and end user, purchaser and attendee,
subscriber and household member, procurer and practitioner — but the structure is the same
whenever "who decides" and "who experiences the result" are not the same person.

**The person who pays** is usually solving a job like: justify this spend, be confident it won't
create a problem for them personally, be able to explain the choice to whoever they answer to. Their
objections cluster around risk, cost, and accountability — will this work, will I regret approving
it, can I defend this decision later.

**The person who consumes** is solving a different job entirely: will this actually be good, will
I want to be here or use this, does this fit what I actually need day to day. Their objections
cluster around fit and experience, not risk and accountability, because the money isn't theirs to
account for.

**A brief that writes one persona covering both** produces copy that persuades neither, because it
either argues risk-and-accountability at someone who doesn't feel that pressure, or argues
fit-and-experience at someone who needs a cost-and-risk case to bring to whoever they answer to. The
page ends up making an argument that lands with nobody in the room.

**What this means for the brief:** whenever the ICP's buying structure separates the person who
decides from the person who experiences the outcome, write them as two personas with two separate
job statements, not one persona with a longer bio. Note in the Personas section which one the page
is primarily written for and how the other's objections get addressed — sometimes on the same page
in a clearly separate section, sometimes by design (a forwardable summary, a one-pager) rather than
by trying to make one continuous argument serve both jobs.

This is not a claim that every offer has this split — plenty of purchases are decided and consumed
by the same person, and forcing a two-persona structure onto a single-decider offer just adds
noise. Check whether the split is real for this ICP before writing two personas; the ICP section's
buying-structure field and the register's `Signals it's the right pick` subsections (see
`register-profiles.md`) are usually enough to tell.

## Ranking personas

Where more than one persona exists, rank them the way the template ranks problems — primary,
secondary, tertiary — by who the page's main argument is written for, not by who's most senior.
The primary persona is whoever's objections the page's structure has to resolve to get a yes; a
secondary persona is addressed but not structurally centered, often through a specific section, a
downloadable asset, or a line built to be forwarded rather than through the whole page's argument.
